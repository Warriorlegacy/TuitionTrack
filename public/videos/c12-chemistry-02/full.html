Introduction: Real-world Applications, Importance of Electrochemistry, and Electrochemical Cells

• Electrochemistry: Study of relationship between electricity and chemical change
• Spontaneous reactions produce electricity (e.g., discharging batteries)
• Non-spontaneous reactions require electricity (e.g., electroplating)
• Oxidation is loss of electrons; Reduction is gain of electrons
• Key components: Redox reactions, Electrochemical cells, and Electrode potential

Electrochemical Cells: Galvanic/Voltaic Cells, Electrode Potential, and Standard Hydrogen Electrode

• Galvanic cells convert spontaneous chemical energy into electrical energy.
• Anode is the site of oxidation; Cathode is the site of reduction.
• The salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells.
• Electrode potential is the tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons.
• Standard Hydrogen Electrode (S.H.E.) is the reference with zero potential.

Nernst Equation: Derivation, Applications, and Calculation of Cell Potential

• Nernst Equation relates cell potential to ion concentrations
• Formula: E = E standard - (RT/nF) ln Q
• Simplified at 25°C: E = E standard - (0.0592/n) log Q
• Cell potential increases as reactant concentration increases
• At equilibrium, E = 0, allowing calculation of Equilibrium Constant K

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

Relationship between Gibbs Free Energy, Equilibrium Constant, and Cell Potential

• Maximum electrical work equals the decrease in Gibbs Free Energy
• Delta G equals minus n times F times E
• At equilibrium, cell potential E is zero and Delta G is zero
• Relationship between E standard and K: E standard equals minus RT/nF ln K
• Delta G standard equals R times T times ln K

Conductance in Electrolytic Solutions: Resistance, Resistivity, Conductivity, and Molar Conductivity

• Resistance (R) is measured in Ohms and depends on electrode geometry.
• Resistivity (rho) is the reciprocal of conductivity.
• Conductivity (kappa) is the ability of a solution to conduct electricity.
• Cell Constant = Distance between electrodes / Area of electrodes.
• Molar Conductivity increases with dilution as volume increases.

Variation of Molar Conductivity with Concentration: Kohlrausch's Law and its Applications

• Strong electrolytes: Linear increase in molar conductivity with dilution
• Weak electrolytes: Sharp increase in molar conductivity at high dilution
• Kohlrausch's Law: Limiting conductivity is the sum of individual ionic conductivities
• Application 1: Calculating limiting molar conductivity for weak electrolytes
• Application 2: Determining degree of dissociation and dissociation constants

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Electrolysis: Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis and Quantitative Aspects

• Faraday's First Law: Mass is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed (m ∝ Q)
• Faraday's Second Law: Mass is proportional to the chemical equivalent weight
• Equivalent Weight = Molar Mass / n-factor (number of electrons transferred)
• Faraday's Constant (F) ≈ 96,485 Coulombs per mole of electrons
• Formula: Mass (m) = (Equivalent Weight × I × t) / F

Products of Electrolysis: Factors Influencing Electrolysis and Electrolytic Cells vs Galvanic Cells

• Product selection depends on standard reduction potentials
• Higher reduction potential means easier discharge
• Concentration and electrode material also influence products
• Galvanic cells: Spontaneous reactions, produce electricity
• Electrolytic cells: Non-spontaneous reactions, consume electricity

Batteries and Fuel Cells: Primary, Secondary, and the Mechanism of Hydrogen-Oxygen Fuel Cell

• Primary Batteries: Irreversible reactions; cannot be recharged (e.g., Dry Cell).
• Secondary Batteries: Reversible reactions; can be recharged (e.g., Lead Storage Battery).
• Lead Storage Battery: Anode is Lead; Cathode is Lead Dioxide; Electrolyte is H2SO4.
• Fuel Cells: Continuous energy production from external fuel supply.
• H2-O2 Fuel Cell: Efficiently converts H2 and O2 into water with minimal pollution.

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Corrosion: Electrochemical Theory, Prevention, and Real-world Case Studies

• Corrosion is an electrochemical process involving anodic and cathodic sites.
• Anode: Iron oxidizes to iron ions and electrons.
• Cathode: Oxygen is reduced to water in the presence of moisture.
• Sacrificial Protection: Using more reactive metals like Zinc to protect Iron.
• Cathodic Protection: Connecting metal to a more active metal to prevent oxidation.

Problem-Solving Workshop: Worked Examples, Word Problems, and Common Calculation Mistakes

• Always identify Anode (oxidation) and Cathode (reduction) first.
• E-cell calculation: E-cathode minus E-anode (watch the signs!).
• Nernst Equation: Use the correct 'n' from the balanced redox equation.
• Common Error: Forgetting to square/cube concentrations in the log term.
• Faraday's Law: Always convert milliamperes to Amperes before calculating.
