Introduction: The Power of Electricity and Real-World Applications

• Electricity: A fundamental form of energy driven by moving charges.
• Static Electricity: Charges at rest (e.g., rubbing a comb on hair).
• Current Electricity: The continuous flow of charge through a conductor.
• Real-world impact: Powering everything from lights to complex computers.
• Chapter Roadmap: From electric charge to complex circuit analysis.

Electric Charge, Coulomb's Law, and the Concept of Current

• Electric charge comes in two types: Positive and Negative.
• Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
• Coulomb's Law: Force is proportional to product of charges and inversely to distance squared.
• Electric Current (I) is the rate of flow of charge (I = Q / t).
• The SI unit of charge is Coulomb (C) and current is Ampere (A).

Electric Potential and Potential Difference: Definitions and Units

• Electric Potential: Work done per unit charge to bring it from infinity.
• Formula: V = W / Q (Potential = Work done / Charge).
• Potential Difference: Work done to move a unit charge between two points.
• SI Unit: The Volt (V), where 1 Volt = 1 Joule / 1 Coulomb.
• A potential difference is essential to drive the flow of electric current.

Circuit Diagrams, Symbols, and the Flow of Electrons

• Standardized symbols allow universal communication in electrical engineering.
• Cell: Long line is positive (+), short thick line is negative (-).
• Closed circuit allows current flow; open circuit breaks the path.
• Conventional Current flows from Positive to Negative terminals.
• Actual Electron Flow moves from Negative to Positive terminals.

Ohm's Law: Mathematical Relationship and Graphical Representation

• Ohm's Law: Current (I) is directly proportional to Potential Difference (V)
• Mathematical Formula: V = I × R
• Resistance (R) is the constant of proportionality measured in Ohms (Ω)
• V-I Graph: A straight line passing through the origin indicates Ohm's Law
• The slope of a V-I graph represents the Resistance (R)

Resistance and Resistivity: Factors Affecting Electrical Resistance

• Resistance (R) is the opposition to the flow of electric current.
• SI unit of Resistance is the Ohm (Ω).
• Resistance is directly proportional to length (L) and inversely to area (A).
• Resistivity (ρ) is a material-specific property independent of shape.
• Formula: Resistance (R) = Resistivity (ρ) × Length (L) / Area (A).

Series Combinations of Resistors: Derivations and Circuit Analysis

• In series, the current (I) remains constant through all resistors.
• Total Voltage (V) is the sum of individual voltages: V = V1 + V2 + V3.
• Derivation: Rs = R1 + R2 + R3.
• Equivalent resistance is always greater than the largest individual resistor.
• If one component fails in series, the entire circuit breaks.

Parallel Combinations of Resistors: Derivations and Circuit Analysis

• In parallel, the potential difference (V) remains constant across all resistors.
• Total current (I) is the sum of individual branch currents: I = I1 + I2 + I3.
• Formula: 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3.
• Equivalent resistance (Rp) is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
• Parallel circuits allow independent operation of electrical appliances.

Heating Effect of Electric Current and Joule's Law of Heating

• Electric current produces heat due to collisions between electrons and atoms.
• Joule's Law: Heat is proportional to the square of current (I squared).
• Joule's Law: Heat is proportional to resistance (R) and time (t).
• Mathematical Formula: H = I squared times R times t.
• Applications: Electric heaters, irons, and light bulb filaments.

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Electric Power: Formulas, Units, and Commercial Unit of Energy

• Electric Power (P) = Rate of doing electrical work = Energy (E) / Time (t)
• SI Unit of Power: Watt (W). 1 Watt = 1 Joule / 1 second
• Three Formulas: P = V × I, P = I² × R, and P = V² / R
• Commercial Unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh). 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10^6 Joules
• Energy (kWh) = Power (in kW) × Time (in hours)

Complex Circuit Solving: Mixed Combinations and Word Problems

• Use the 'Reduction Method' to simplify complex circuits step-by-step.
• Identify small sub-circuits (series or parallel) and replace them with one value.
• Convert word problems into circuit diagrams by identifying key phrases.
• In parallel branches, Voltage remains constant; in series, Current remains constant.
• Always find the total equivalent resistance before calculating total current.
