Introduction: The Language of Physics, Importance of Measurement, and Physical Quantities

• Physics relies on precise, quantitative observations rather than qualitative guesses.
• Measurement: The process of quantifying physical properties using numbers and units.
• Physical Quantity: A property of a material or system that can be measured.
• The structure of a measurement: Numerical value (n) multiplied by a Unit (u).
• Formula representation: q = n × u (e.g., Length = 5 meters).

The International System of Units (SI): Base vs. Derived Units and Prefixes

• SI System: A standardized global language for scientific measurement.
• 7 Base Units: Meter, Kilogram, Second, Ampere, Kelvin, Mole, Candela.
• Derived Units: Units created by combining base units (e.g., m/s, Newton).
• SI Prefixes: Using powers of ten to handle very large or very small scales.
• Scale Management: From Tera (10^12) down to Pico (10^-12).

Measurement of Length, Mass, and Time: Large and Small Scales (Parallax Method)

• Parallax: Apparent shift in object position when viewed from different angles
• Parallax Formula: Distance equals Basis divided by Parallax Angle (in radians)
• Mass: Measured via balances in labs or mass spectrometry for subatomic scales
• Time: Measured from stopwatches to high-precision Cesium atomic clocks
• Scale Matters: Measurement tools must match the magnitude of the quantity

Dimensional Analysis: Concept of Dimensions and Dimensional Formulae

• Dimensions represent the nature of a physical quantity.
• Base dimensions: Mass [M], Length [L], and Time [T].
• Dimensional Formula: Expressing quantities as products of base dimensions.
• Use negative exponents for quantities in the denominator (e.g., T to the power of -1).
• Example: Force = M L T to the power of -2.

Applications of Dimensional Analysis: Checking Homogeneity and Deriving Relations

• Principle of Homogeneity: Only quantities with same dimensions can be added/subtracted
• Dimensional consistency: Every term in an equation must have identical dimensions
• Application 1: Verifying the correctness of physical equations
• Application 2: Deriving formulas by relating physical quantities and their powers
• Limitation: Cannot determine dimensionless constants or handle transcendental functions

Limitations of Dimensional Analysis and Error in Dimensional Consistency

• Cannot determine dimensionless constants like 2 pi or 1/2
• Fails if a quantity depends on more than three fundamental dimensions
• Cannot handle trigonometric, logarithmic, or exponential functions
• Does not distinguish between scalar and vector quantities
• Dimensional consistency is necessary but not sufficient for correctness

Significant Figures: Rules, Arithmetic Operations, and Precision vs. Accuracy

• Significant figures: Reliable digits plus one uncertain digit
• Rules: Non-zeros are significant; trapped zeros are significant
• Leading zeros are placeholders; trailing zeros with decimals are significant
• Addition/Subtraction: Limit by the least number of decimal places
• Multiplication/Division: Limit by the least number of significant figures

Error Analysis I: Absolute, Relative, and Percentage Errors

• Error: The difference between the true value and the measured value.
• Absolute Error: The magnitude of the difference between true and measured values.
• Relative Error: The ratio of mean absolute error to the true value.
• Percentage Error: The relative error expressed as a percentage.
• Precision is determined by how small the percentage error is.

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

Error Analysis II: Combination of Errors in Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, and Division

• Addition and Subtraction: Add the absolute errors (delta Z = delta A + delta B)
• Errors always accumulate; they never cancel out during addition or subtraction
• Multiplication and Division: Add the relative errors (delta Z / Z = delta A / A + delta B / B)
• Percentage Error Rule: Sum of individual percentage errors for product or quotient
• Always use absolute errors for sums and relative errors for products/quotients

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Error Analysis III: Propagation of Errors in Powers and Complex Formulas

• Power Rule: Relative error in Z = n * (Relative error in X) if Z = X^n
• For Z = X^a * Y^b / W^c, total error is a(dX/X) + b(dY/Y) + c(dW/W)
• Constants like pi or 4/3 do not contribute to error propagation
• Always add errors to find the maximum possible uncertainty
• Percentage error is the total relative error multiplied by 100

Numerical Mastery: Worked Examples, Word Problems, and Common Calculation Pitfalls

• Error propagation: Multiply relative error by the power of the variable
• Significant figures in addition: Match the least number of decimal places
• Avoid rounding intermediate steps to prevent cumulative errors
• Always standardize units before performing error arithmetic
• Relative error in square roots: Use a multiplier of one-half
