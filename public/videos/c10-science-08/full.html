Introduction: Why Heredity Matters & The Concept of Variation

• Heredity: The transmission of traits from parents to offspring.
• Variation: The differences existing between individuals of a species.
• Sexual reproduction leads to high levels of genetic variation.
• Asexual reproduction produces clones with minor accidental variations.
• Variation is essential for the survival and evolution of a species.

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

Mendel's Laws: Monohybrid Cross & Law of Segregation

• Dominant alleles (T) mask recessive alleles (t)
• Homozygous (TT/tt) vs. Heterozygous (Tt) genotypes
• F-one generation: All offspring are heterozygous (Tt)
• Law of Segregation: Alleles separate during gamete formation
• F-two Phenotypic Ratio is 3:1; Genotypic Ratio is 1:2:1

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

Dihybrid Cross: Law of Independent Assortment & Punnett Squares

• Dihybrid cross: Studying the inheritance of two different traits simultaneously.
• F-one generation: All offspring show dominant phenotypes (Round and Yellow).
• Law of Independent Assortment: Traits are inherited independently of each other.
• Gamete formation: RrYy produces four types: RY, Ry, rY, and ry.
• F-two phenotypic ratio: 9 (Round-Yellow) : 3 (Round-Green) : 3 (Wrinkled-Yellow) : 1 (Wrinkled-Green).

Mathematical Analysis: Phenotypic vs Genotypic Ratios & Probability

• Genotype: The genetic makeup (e.g., TT, Tt, tt)
• Phenotype: The physical appearance (e.g., Tall, Dwarf)
• Monohybrid Ratio: Genotypic 1:2:1 | Phenotypic 3:1
• Dihybrid Phenotypic Ratio: 9:3:3:1
• Product Rule: Multiply probabilities for 'AND' events

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Non-Mendelian Inheritance: Incomplete Dominance & Co-dominance

• Mendel's Law: One allele completely masks the recessive allele.
• Incomplete Dominance: Alleles blend to form an intermediate phenotype (e.g., Pink flowers).
• Incomplete Dominance Ratio: F-two phenotypic ratio is 1:2:1.
• Co-dominance: Both alleles express themselves equally and distinctly.
• Co-dominance Example: ABO Blood Group (Type AB shows both A and B antigens).

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Sex Determination in Humans: Chromosomes and Biological Sex

• Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes.
• Biological females have two X chromosomes (X X), while males have X and Y (X Y).
• Females always contribute an X chromosome to the offspring.
• Males contribute either an X or a Y chromosome via sperm cells.
• The sex of the child is determined by the type of sperm that fertilizes the egg.

The Mechanism of Inheritance: DNA, Genes, and Chromosomes

• Chromosomes: Thread-like structures in the nucleus containing genetic material.
• DNA: Deoxyribonucleic Acid, the molecule that carries genetic instructions.
• Genes: Specific segments of DNA that code for particular traits.
• Histones: Proteins that help in the tight packaging of DNA into chromosomes.
• Locus: The specific physical location of a gene on a chromosome.

Common Pitfalls: Misconceptions in Genetics & Terminology Errors

• Phenotype is physical appearance; Genotype is the genetic makeup (e.g., Tt).
• Dominant traits are not necessarily more common in a population.
• Acquired traits (like muscle growth) are NOT passed to offspring.
• Alleles are specific versions of a gene; they do not blend during gamete formation.
• Pure-bred means homozygous; Hybrid means heterozygous.

Problem-Solving Workshop: Solving Heredity Word Problems & Pedigree Charts

• Step 2: Use Punnett squares to calculate Genotypic and Phenotypic ratios.
• Step 3: Square = Male; Circle = Female; Shaded = Trait expressed.
• Step 4: If a trait skips a generation, it is likely recessive.
• Step 5: If a trait appears in every generation, it is likely dominant.
