• Data Collection: Gathering information through surveys or observations.
• Raw Data: Information in its original, unorganized form.
• Organization: Arranging data systematically to make it useful.
• Frequency: The number of times a particular observation occurs.

Representing Data: Pictographs and Interpreting Scales

• Pictographs use symbols or pictures to represent data visually.
• A Scale (Key) defines the value represented by a single symbol.
• Use a scale to represent large numbers without drawing too many icons.
• Partial symbols (like half a picture) represent fractional values.
• Always check the 'Key' first before interpreting any pictograph.

Bar Graphs: Construction, Scaling, and Visual Analysis

• Bar graphs use rectangular bars of equal width to represent data.
• The x-axis represents categories and the y-axis represents numerical values.
• Scaling is choosing a unit value to represent a range of numbers.
• Ensure uniform width for bars and uniform gaps between them.
• Visual analysis allows for quick comparison and trend identification.

Double Bar Graphs: Comparing Two Sets of Data

• Double bar graphs compare two sets of data side-by-side.
• Use a 'Key' or 'Legend' to distinguish between the two data sets.
• The x-axis represents categories; the y-axis represents values.
• Bars for the same category must be adjacent (touching).
• Gaps should exist between different category pairs for clarity.

Introduction to Central Tendency: The Concept of Average

• Data can be overwhelming when looking at large lists of numbers.
• Central Tendency finds a single value that represents the whole group.
• The 'Center' is the typical value around which data clusters.
• An 'Average' summarizes data into one representative number.
• We will learn three main tools: Mean, Mode, and Median.

Part 7 of 12 · ~4 min

The Arithmetic Mean: Formula, Calculation, and Worked Examples

• Arithmetic Mean is the sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations.
• Formula: Mean = (Sum of all observations) / (Total number of observations)
• Step 1: Add all the values in the data set to find the total sum.
• Step 2: Count the total number of values present in the set.
• The mean provides a single representative value for the entire data set.

The Mode: Finding the Most Frequent Observation

• Mode: The observation that occurs most frequently in a data set.
• Bimodal: A data set with two modes.
• Multimodal: A data set with more than two modes.
• No Mode: Occurs when all observations appear with equal frequency.
• Tip: Arrange data in ascending order to count frequencies easily.

A 3D animation of colorful numbered spheres falling into transparent bins, where the bin with the most spheres glows brightly to represent the mode.

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

The Median: Arranging Data and Finding the Middle Value

• Step 1: Always arrange data in ascending or descending order first.
• Median is the middle value of an ordered data set.
• If the number of observations is odd, find the single middle value.
• If the number of observations is even, find the mean of the two middle values.
• The median is not affected by extreme values in the data set.

Part 10 of 12 · ~4 min

Range and Data Spread: Understanding Variability and Extremes

• Mean tells us the center, but Range tells us the spread.
• Range Formula: Range = Maximum Value - Minimum Value.
• Small Range = Consistent and stable data.
• Large Range = High variability and unpredictable data.
• Range measures the distance between the extremes.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Complex Word Problems: Integrating Mean, Median, and Mode

• Identify whether the problem asks for Mean, Median, or Mode.
• Always arrange data in ascending order before finding the Median.
• Use the formula: Sum of observations = Mean × Number of observations.
• Compare Mean, Median, and Mode to interpret the data story.
• Check that your final answer includes the correct units.
