Introduction: The Significance of the National Movement and the Rise of Nationalism

• The National Movement shaped the modern, democratic India we live in today.
• Colonial rule unintentionally provided tools for national unification.
• Modern education introduced ideas of liberty, equality, and democracy.
• Nationalism emerged as a reaction to British economic and political exploitation.
• A shared sense of grievance transformed diverse groups into a unified nation.

The Emergence of Political Associations and Early Agitation (1885-1905)

• Rise of regional groups like Poona Sarvajanik Sabha and Indian Association
• Formation of the Indian National Congress in Bombay, December 1885
• First Congress session presided over by Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee
• Focus on constitutional methods: petitions, speeches, and resolutions
• Early movement led by the urban, educated elite rather than the masses

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

The Moderate Phase: Ideology, Methods, and the Early Congress

• First INC session: Bombay, 1885, presided by W.C. Bonnerjee
• Moderate Leaders: Dadabhai Naoroji, P.M. Mehta, and G.K. Gokhale
• Methodology: The 3-P approach—Prayer, Petition, and Protest
• Economic Critique: Dadabhai Naoroji's 'Drain of Wealth' theory
• Primary Goal: Constitutional reforms and increased Indian representation

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

The Extremist Phase: Swadeshi, Boycott, and the Partition of Bengal

• Partition of Bengal (1905) by Lord Curzon to weaken nationalist unity.
• Rise of Extremist leaders: Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, and Bipin Chandra Pal.
• The slogan 'Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it!' by Tilak.
• Swadeshi: Using indigenous goods; Boycott: Rejecting British imports.
• Shift from elite petitions to mass-based political agitation.

A 3D map of 1905 Bengal splitting into two colors, transitioning into a montage of protesters burning foreign cloth and the Lal-Bal-Pal trio appearing in stylized portraits.

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

The Rise of Revolutionary Nationalism and Early Mass Movements

• Rise of secret societies like Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar in Bengal.
• Revolutionary ideology: Direct action and armed struggle against British rule.
• Key figures: Khudiram Bose, Prafulla Chaki, and the Chapekar brothers.
• Limitations: Localized impact and lack of connection with the rural masses.
• Transition period: Moving from individual heroism to organized mass movements.

• Gandhi's return to India in 1915 and the concept of Satyagraha
• Early successes: Champaran, Kheda, and Ahmedabad struggles
• The Khilafat Movement and the goal of Hindu-Muslim unity
• Non-Cooperation Movement: Boycott of schools, courts, and foreign cloth

The Civil Disobedience Movement: Salt March and Mass Mobilization

• 1929 Lahore Session: Declaration of Purna Swaraj (Complete Independence)
• The Salt Tax: A symbol of colonial exploitation affecting all classes
• The Dandi March: 240-mile journey from Sabarmati to the coast
• Civil Disobedience: Breaking laws vs. merely refusing cooperation
• Mass Mobilization: Large-scale participation of women and peasants

• The Quit India Movement of 1942 and Gandhi's 'Do or Die' slogan.
• Spontaneous mass uprisings following the arrest of Congress leaders.
• Subhash Chandra Bose and the formation of the Azad Hind Fauj (INA).

Social Reformers, Communalism, and the Divide and Rule Policy

• Social reformers fought against Sati, casteism, and for women's education.
• Communalism prioritizes religious identity over national unity.
• The British 'Divide and Rule' policy aimed to weaken the nationalist movement.
• Separate Electorates were used to deepen religious political divisions.
• The tension between unity and partition shaped the final years of struggle.
