Introduction: Why AC Matters, Real-world Applications, and the Concept of Time-Varying Quantities

• DC flows in one direction; AC reverses direction periodically
• AC allows efficient long-distance power transmission via transformers
• High voltage transmission reduces energy loss due to resistance
• AC quantities are functions of time, often represented by sine waves
• Frequency is the rate at which the current completes one cycle

Alternating Voltage and Current: Mathematical Representation, RMS and Peak Values, and Phase

• Mathematical Form: V = V naught sin(omega t)
• Peak Value (V naught): Maximum amplitude of the wave
• RMS Value: V-R-M-S = V naught / sqrt(2) ≈ 0.707 V naught
• Effective Value: RMS is the value used for practical calculations
• Phase and Phasors: Representing timing and direction using rotating vectors

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

AC through a Purely Resistive Circuit: Waveforms, Phase Relationships, and Power Dissipation

• Instantaneous Current: I = I naught sin(omega T)
• Phase Relationship: Voltage and Current are 'in phase' (phi = 0)
• Waveform: V and I sine waves are perfectly synchronized
• Average Power Formula: P-avg = V-rms times I-rms
• Energy: All electrical energy is dissipated as heat

AC through a Purely Inductive Circuit: Inductive Reactance, Phase Lag, and Energy Storage

• Induced EMF opposes change in current (Lenz's Law)
• Current lags voltage by 90 degrees (pi/2 phase lag)
• Inductive Reactance: X-L = omega times L
• Reactance increases with frequency
• Energy is stored in the magnetic field, not dissipated

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

AC through a Purely Capacitive Circuit: Capacitive Reactance, Phase Lead, and Energy Storage

• Current leads voltage by 90 degrees (pi/2 phase lead)
• Capacitive Reactance formula: Xc = 1 / (omega * C)
• Xc is inversely proportional to frequency and capacitance
• Capacitors block DC (zero frequency) but pass AC
• Average power dissipation in a pure capacitor is zero

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Series LCR Circuit: Impedance Derivation, Phasor Diagrams, and Resonance Conditions

• In series LCR, current I is common to all components.
• Phasor Diagram: V-L leads I by 90°, V-C lags I by 90°.
• Impedance Formula: Z = sqrt(R² + (X_L - X_C)²)
• Resonance Condition: X_L = X_C (Inductive = Capacitive Reactance)
• Resonant Frequency: f₀ = 1 / (2π * sqrt(LC))

LCR Resonance: Sharpness of Resonance (Q-factor), Bandwidth, and Practical Applications

• Sharpness of Resonance: How quickly current drops away from resonant frequency.
• Quality Factor (Q-factor): Ratio of resonant frequency to bandwidth (Q = omega naught * L / R).
• Bandwidth: The range of frequencies where current is at least 71% of maximum.
• High Q-factor implies a narrow bandwidth and high selectivity.
• Practical Application: Tuning circuits in Radio and Television receivers.

A 3D animated graph showing a sharp current-frequency curve versus a flat curve, with highlighted regions for bandwidth and peak height.

Part 8 of 12 · ~5 min

Power in AC Circuits: Power Factor, Wattless Current, and Average Power Calculations

• Instantaneous Power: P = V(t) * I(t)
• Average Power: P-avg = V-rms * I-rms * cos(phi)
• Power Factor: cos(phi) = R / Z
• Wattless Current: Current where average power is zero
• Resonance: Power factor is maximum (equals 1) at resonance

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

LC Oscillations: Energy Transfer between L and C, Damping Effects, and Mathematical Modeling

• Energy oscillates between Capacitor (Electric Field) and Inductor (Magnetic Field)
• Mathematical model: L times d-squared-Q/dt-squared plus Q/C equals zero
• Angular frequency of oscillation: omega equals square root of one divided by LC
• Real-world Damping: Resistance converts electrical energy into heat
• Damped oscillations show an exponential decay in amplitude over time

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Transformers: Principle of Mutual Induction, Step-up/Step-down, Efficiency, and Energy Loss

• Principle: Mutual Induction between two coils via a magnetic core
• Transformer Ratio: E-s / E-p = N-s / N-p
• Step-up: N-s > N-p (Increases Voltage); Step-down: N-s < N-p (Decreases Voltage)
• Efficiency (eta): Ratio of Output Power to Input Power
• Energy Losses: Copper loss, Eddy currents, Hysteresis, and Flux leakage

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Common Pitfalls: Conceptual Errors in Phase, Reactance, and RMS vs Peak Value Calculations

• Always assume given voltage/current is RMS unless 'peak' is specified.
• Use Z = square root of (R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2), never add R, X_L, and X_C linearly.
• Reactance depends on frequency: X_L increases with f, X_C decreases with f.
• Power in AC is P = V-rms times I-rms times cos(phi).
• Use phasor diagrams to determine if current leads or lags in LCR circuits.
