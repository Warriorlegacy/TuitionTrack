Introduction: Real-world Applications, Importance, and Classification of Haloalkanes & Haloarenes

• Haloalkanes: Halogen attached to an sp3 hybridized carbon atom.
• Haloarenes: Halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring.
• Classification by number: Mono, Di, Tri, and Poly-haloalkanes.
• Classification by carbon type: Primary (1°), Secondary (2°), and Tertiary (3°).
• Special types: Allylic halides (adjacent to C=C) and Vinylic halides (on C=C).

Nomenclature and Structure: IUPAC Rules, Isomerism, and Molecular Geometry

• IUPAC Naming: Identify longest chain and assign lowest locant to halogen
• Haloarene Naming: Use ortho, meta, para for benzene ring substituents
• Molecular Geometry: sp3 hybridization leads to tetrahedral shape
• Structural Isomerism: Includes chain and position isomerism
• Stereoisomerism: Chiral centers lead to non-superimposable enantiomers

Methods of Preparation I: From Alcohols, Hydrocarbons, and Halogen Exchange

• Alcohols to Haloalkanes: Using HX, PX3, or PX5 reagents
• Lucas Reagent: HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2 for alcohol classification
• Alkenes to Haloalkanes: Electrophilic addition following Markovnikov's Rule
• Alkanes to Haloalkanes: Free radical substitution via UV light
• Halogen Exchange: Finkelstein reaction for Iodides and Swarts for Fluorides

Methods of Preparation II: Electrophilic Substitution and Specialized Synthetic Routes

• Gattermann Reaction: An alternative route using copper powder and HX

Physical Properties: Boiling Points, Solubility, and Density Trends

• Boiling point increases with molecular mass and decreases with branching
• Van der Waals forces are weaker in branched isomers due to less surface area
• Haloalkanes are insoluble in water because they cannot form hydrogen bonds
• Haloalkanes are highly soluble in organic solvents like ether and acetone
• Density increases as the atomic mass of the halogen atom increases

Chemical Properties I: Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions (SN1 vs SN2 Mechanisms)

• SN2: Single-step, bimolecular, backside attack, and Walden Inversion.
• SN2 Reactivity: Methyl > Primary > Secondary > Tertiary (due to sterics).
• SN1: Two-step, unimolecular, involves a carbocation intermediate.
• SN1 Reactivity: Tertiary > Secondary > Primary (due to carbocation stability).
• Stereochemical outcome: SN2 leads to inversion; SN1 leads to racemization.

Chemical Properties II: Stereochemistry, Optical Activity, Chirality, and Racemization

• Chirality: Non-superimposable mirror images (like hands)
• Chiral Carbon: A carbon atom bonded to four different groups
• Optical Activity: Rotation of plane-polarized light (+ or -)
• Enantiomers: Pairs of non-superimposable mirror-image molecules
• Racemization: Formation of a 50-50 mixture of enantiomers

Chemical Properties III: Elimination Reactions (Saytzeff's Rule) and Reaction with Metals

• Dehydrohalogenation: Removal of hydrogen halide to form alkenes.
• Saytzeff's Rule: More substituted alkenes are the major products.
• Grignard Reagent: Formation of R-Mg-X using Magnesium in dry ether.
• Wurtz Reaction: Sodium reacts with alkyl halides to form higher alkanes.
• Wurtz-Fittig & Fittig Reactions: Coupling of alkyl and aryl halides.

Chemical Properties IV: Reactions of Haloarenes (Electrophilic Substitution and Nucleophilic Substitution)

• Resonance increases C-X bond strength in haloarenes
• Halogens are ortho-para directing in electrophilic substitution
• Major reactions: Halogenation, Nitration, Sulphonation, Friedel-Crafts
• Haloarenes are less reactive to nucleophiles due to sp2 hybridization
• Electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para positions increase reactivity

Nucleophilic Substitution in Haloarenes: Effect of Substituents and Mechanism Nuances

• Resonance effect creates partial double bond character in C-X bond
• sp2 hybridization makes C-X bond stronger and shorter than sp3
• Electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) increase reactivity at ortho/para positions
• Mechanism: Addition-Elimination via a Meisenheimer complex
• Dow Process: High T and P required for chlorobenzene to phenol conversion

Part 11 of 12 · ~3 min

Polyhalogen Compounds: Environmental Impact, Uses, and Safety Profiles

• Uses: Chloroform as solvent, Freons in refrigeration, Iodoform as antiseptic
• Environmental Risk: Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) cause ozone layer depletion
• Bioaccumulation: Lipophilic compounds build up in the fatty tissues of organisms
• Biomagnification: Increasing concentration of toxins up the food chain
• Safety: Many polyhalogen compounds are volatile, toxic, or carcinogenic

Exam Masterclass: Problem-Solving Strategies, Common Mistakes, and Full Chapter Recap

• Strategy: Always identify the nucleophile strength and electrophile site first.
• Common Error: Confusing carbocation stability with SN2 feasibility.
• Solvent Tip: Polar protic favors SN1; Polar aprotic favors SN2.
• Elimination: Saytzeff's Rule favors the more substituted alkene.
• Haloarenes: Low reactivity due to resonance and sp2 hybridization.
