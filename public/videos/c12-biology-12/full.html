Ecosystem Structure: Components, Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers

• Ecosystem components: Abiotic (non-living) and Biotic (living)
• Producers (Autotrophs): Convert solar energy into organic food
• Consumers (Heterotrophs): Primary (herbivores), Secondary, and Tertiary
• Decomposers (Saprotrophs): Break down dead organic matter
• Nutrient recycling: Decomposers return inorganic nutrients to the environment

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

Ecosystem Functions I: Productivity (Primary, Secondary, and Net)

• Productivity: The rate of biomass production in an ecosystem.
• Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): Total organic matter produced by autotrophs.
• Net Primary Productivity (NPP): NPP = GPP minus Respiration (R).
• NPP is the actual biomass available to consumers.
• Secondary Productivity: Rate of biomass formation by consumers/heterotrophs.

A split-screen 3D animation: Left side shows a plant absorbing sunlight (GPP), a small portion of energy escaping as heat (R), and the remaining energy building a fruit (NPP); Right side shows a herbivore eating the fruit and converting it into body mass (Secondary Productivity).

Ecosystem Functions II: Decomposition (Steps, Factors, and Rate)

• Decomposition: Breakdown of complex organic matter into simple inorganic substances
• Steps: Fragmentation, Leaching, Catabolism, Humification, and Mineralization
• Humus: Dark, nutrient-rich, and resistant substance formed during decomposition
• Factors: Detritus quality (Nitrogen vs Lignin/Chitin) and Physicochemical conditions
• Rate: Increased by warmth, moisture, and high nitrogen content

A 3D time-lapse animation showing a fallen leaf transforming into soil particles, with glowing icons representing nitrogen and moisture moving through the layers.

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

Ecosystem Functions III: Energy Flow and Trophic Levels

• Energy flow is unidirectional: Sunlight to Producers to Consumers
• First Law of Thermodynamics: Energy transformation occurs
• Second Law of Thermodynamics: Energy is lost as heat at each step
• The Ten Percent Law: Only ten percent of energy transfers to the next level
• Energy limitation restricts the number of trophic levels in a food chain

A 3D diagram showing a sun radiating light to green plants, with glowing energy arrows shrinking in size as they move from plants to a deer, and then to a lion, while red heat waves dissipate from each animal.

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Ecosystem Functions IV: Ecological Pyramids (Number, Biomass, and Energy)

• Ecological Pyramids: Graphical representation of trophic level relationships.
• Pyramid of Number: Can be upright (grassland) or inverted (parasitic food chain).
• Pyramid of Biomass: Upright in terrestrial; inverted in aquatic ecosystems.
• Pyramid of Energy: Always upright due to energy loss at each level.
• Key Concept: Energy transfer follows the 10 percent law.

Food Chains and Food Webs: Dynamics and Stability

• Grazing Food Chain (GFC): Starts with living producers and solar energy.
• Detritus Food Chain (DFC): Starts with dead organic matter and decomposers.
• Food Web: A complex network of interconnected food chains.
• Stability: Complexity in food webs provides ecosystem resilience.
• Redundancy: Alternative prey options prevent ecosystem collapse.

A 3D transition from a single straight line of icons (Grass -> Grasshopper -> Frog) to a dense, glowing web of interconnected organism icons.

Ecological Succession I: Primary and Secondary Succession

• Ecological Succession: Gradual change in species composition over time.
• Primary Succession: Starts on bare substrates with no pre-existing soil.
• Pioneer Species in Primary Succession: Lichens and mosses that build soil.
• Secondary Succession: Occurs in disturbed areas where soil is already present.
• Rate of Change: Secondary succession is significantly faster than primary.

A split-screen 3D animation: Left side shows lava cooling into bare rock with lichens slowly spreading; Right side shows a burnt forest floor with green sprouts rapidly emerging from dark soil.

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

Ecological Succession II: Sere, Climax Community, and Detailed Processes

• Sere: The entire sequence of ecological communities in an area.
• Seral Stage: Each individual community during the succession process.
• Pioneer Community: The first hardy species to colonize a bare area.
• Facilitation: Process where one community modifies the environment for the next.
• Climax Community: The stable, self-sustaining final stage determined by climate.

A time-lapse 3D animation showing bare rock transforming into lichens, then grasses, shrubs, and finally a dense, stable forest.

Nutrient Cycling I: The Phosphorus Cycle

• Phosphorus is a sedimentary cycle with no significant gaseous phase.
• Essential components: DNA, RNA, ATP, and Phospholipids.
• Primary reservoir: Earth's crust and phosphate-bearing rocks.
• Process: Weathering -> Plant Uptake -> Consumption -> Decomposition.
• Long-term storage: Ocean sediments and geological uplift.

A 3D cross-section of an ecosystem showing a mountain weathering into soil, plants absorbing ions, animals eating, and phosphorus particles washing into a deep ocean trench to form rock layers.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Nutrient Cycling II: The Nitrogen Cycle (Fixation to Denitrification)

• Nitrogen makes up 78% of the atmosphere but is unusable in its N-two gas form.
• Nitrogen Fixation: Converting N-two into ammonia via bacteria or lightning.
• Nitrification: Two-step process converting ammonia to nitrites then nitrates.
• Assimilation: Plants absorbing nitrates to build proteins and nucleic acids.
• Denitrification: Bacteria converting nitrates back into N-two gas to complete the cycle.

A continuous 3D circular flow animation showing nitrogen moving from the sky to soil bacteria, into plant roots, through an animal, and back to the atmosphere via specialized soil microbes.
