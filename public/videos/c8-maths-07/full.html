Introduction: Real-world Applications of Comparing Quantities & The Concept of Ratios

• Comparing quantities helps us make informed real-world decisions.
• A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division.
• Always ensure both quantities are in the same units before comparing.
• Ratios are expressed using a colon, for example, a colon b.
• Ratios should always be expressed in their simplest form.

Ratios and Proportions: Deep Dive, Unitary Method, and Comparison Techniques

• Ratios must compare quantities in the same units
• Proportion: When two ratios are equal (a:b = c:d)
• Property: Product of extremes = Product of means
• Unitary Method: Find the value of one unit first
• Comparison: Use unit rates to find the best value

Percentage Fundamentals: Conversions, Fractional Forms, and Visualizing Percentages

• Percent means 'per hundred' or a fraction with a denominator of 100
• To convert % to fraction: Remove % sign and divide by 100
• To convert % to decimal: Move the decimal point two places to the left
• To convert fraction to %: Multiply the fraction by 100
• Visualizing percentages using a 100-square grid or a number line

Finding Increase and Decrease Percentages: Formulas and Step-by-Step Derivations

• Step 1: Calculate the absolute change (New Value - Original Value)
• Step 2: Always divide the change by the ORIGINAL value
• Percentage Increase Formula: (Increase / Original Value) x 100
• Percentage Decrease Formula: (Decrease / Original Value) x 100
• Key Rule: The original value is always the base for calculation

Profit and Loss: Cost Price, Selling Price, Profit/Loss Percent, and Markup Concepts

• Cost Price (CP): Total amount spent to acquire and prepare an item for sale.
• Selling Price (SP): The final amount received when an item is sold.
• Profit = SP - CP (when SP > CP); Loss = CP - SP (when CP > SP).
• Profit/Loss Percentage is always calculated on the Cost Price.
• Markup: The increase applied to the Cost Price to set a higher selling price.

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Discount and Sales Tax (VAT/GST): Calculating Marked Price, Discount, and Final Bill

• Marked Price (M.P.): The price printed on the item tag.
• Discount = Marked Price - Selling Price.
• Discount Percentage = (Discount / Marked Price) x 100.
• GST/Tax is calculated on the Selling Price, not M.P.
• Final Bill = Selling Price + Sales Tax (GST).

Simple Interest: The Concept of Principal, Rate, Time, and Linear Growth

• Principal (P): The original amount of money borrowed or invested.
• Rate (R): The percentage of interest charged per year.
• Time (T): The duration for which the money is borrowed, in years.
• Simple Interest Formula: I = (P × R × T) / 100
• Linear Growth: Interest remains constant each year based on the Principal.

Compound Interest Part 1: The Concept of Compounding and Deriving the Formula

• Simple Interest: Interest is calculated only on the original principal.
• Compound Interest: Interest is added to the principal to form a new principal.
• Compounding: The process of earning interest on previously earned interest.
• Amount Formula: A equals P times (1 plus R divided by 100) raised to the power n.
• Compound Interest Formula: C.I. equals Amount minus Principal.

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

Compound Interest Part 2: Calculating Interest Annually, Half-Yearly, and Quarterly

• Compounding Annually: Rate and Time remain as given in the problem.
• Compounding Half-Yearly: Divide Rate by 2 and multiply Time by 2.
• Compounding Quarterly: Divide Rate by 4 and multiply Time by 4.
• Formula: A = P (1 + r/n)^(n*t) where n is the frequency per year.
• More frequent compounding leads to a higher total Amount.

Applications of Compound Interest: Population Growth and Depreciation Models

• Population growth follows the Compound Interest formula logic
• Initial Population acts as the Principal (P)
• Depreciation means a decrease in value over time
• For Growth: Use (1 + r/100) in the formula
• For Depreciation: Use (1 - r/100) in the formula

Advanced Word Problems: Integrating Multiple Concepts and Avoiding Common Pitfalls

• Follow the chronological flow of transactions to avoid calculation errors.
• Never add successive percentages; use the multiplier method instead.
• Always apply tax or GST to the final discounted price, not the cost price.
• In reverse problems, treat the final amount as a percentage of the original.
• Use the 'Extraction, Sequence, and Reality Check' strategy for complex tasks.
