Introduction: Why Metals and Non-metals Shape Our Modern World

• Elements are the fundamental building blocks of all matter.
• Materials are broadly classified into Metals and Non-metals.
• Metals provide structural strength and conductivity.
• Non-metals are essential for life processes and chemical reactions.

Physical Properties I: Lustre, Malleability, and Ductility

• Lustre: The characteristic shine or reflective property of metals.
• Malleability: The ability to be beaten into thin sheets without breaking.
• Ductility: The property of being drawn or stretched into thin wires.
• Non-metals are typically dull, brittle, and non-ductile.
• Examples: Aluminum foil (malleability) and Copper wiring (ductility).

Physical Properties II: Conductivity, Sonority, and State of Matter

• Metals are excellent conductors of electricity; non-metals are insulators.
• Graphite is a unique non-metal that conducts electricity.
• Metals are sonorous, producing a ringing sound when struck.
• Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature.
• Bromine is the only non-metal that is liquid at room temperature.

Chemical Properties I: Reaction with Oxygen (Oxides)

• Metals react with oxygen to form Metal Oxides.
• Metal oxides are generally basic in nature (turn red litmus blue).
• Non-metals react with oxygen to form Non-metal Oxides.
• Non-metal oxides are generally acidic in nature (turn blue litmus red).
• Example: Magnesium + Oxygen yields Magnesium Oxide (Basic).

Part 5 of 12 · ~4 min

Chemical Properties II: Reaction with Water and Acids

• Metals + Water -> Metal Hydroxide/Oxide + Hydrogen gas
• Sodium and Potassium react violently with cold water
• Iron and Aluminium react only with steam
• Metals + Dilute Acid -> Metal Salt + Hydrogen gas
• The 'Pop Test' confirms the presence of Hydrogen gas

Part 6 of 12 · ~3 min

Chemical Properties III: Reaction with Other Metal Salts (Displacement)

• Displacement: A more reactive metal replaces a less reactive metal in a salt solution.
• Zinc + Copper Sulphate (Blue) -> Zinc Sulphate (Colorless) + Copper (Red-brown).
• Iron + Copper Sulphate (Blue) -> Iron Sulphate (Green) + Copper.
• Reaction only occurs if the single metal is more reactive than the metal in the salt.
• Chemical Rule: Reactive Metal + Less Reactive Salt -> Reactive Salt + Less Reactive Metal.

Part 7 of 12 · ~4 min

The Reactivity Series: Understanding Metal Potency

• Reactivity Series: A ranking of metals from most to least reactive.
• High Reactivity: Potassium and Sodium react violently with water.
• Low Reactivity: Gold and Silver are stable and do not tarnish easily.
• Displacement Rule: A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one.
• Predicting Reactions: Use the series to determine if a reaction will occur.

Metals vs. Non-metals: Comparative Summary and Activity Analysis

• Physical: Lustrous and Malleable vs. Dull and Brittle
• State: Mostly Solids (except Mercury) vs. Solids, Liquids, or Gases
• Chemical: Metals form Basic Oxides; Non-metals form Acidic Oxides
• Reactivity: Metals displace less reactive metals in salt solutions
• Electron Behavior: Metals lose electrons; Non-metals gain electrons

Uses of Metals and Non-metals in Daily Life and Industry

• Metals: Used in construction (Iron/Steel) and electrical wiring (Copper/Aluminum).
• Precious Metals: Gold and Platinum used in jewelry due to lustre and non-corrosion.
• Non-metals: Oxygen for respiration and Nitrogen for fertilizers and food safety.
• Carbon uses: Graphite for lubricants and Diamond for industrial cutting tools.
• Chemical uses: Chlorine for water purification and Sulfur for industrial acids.

Common Mistakes, Misconceptions, and Conceptual Pitfalls

• Mercury is a liquid metal; not all metals are solids.
• Lustre does not guarantee a metal (e.g., Iodine).
• Amphoteric oxides like Aluminum Oxide act as both acid and base.
• Displacement only occurs from more reactive to less reactive metals.
• Graphite is a non-metal that conducts electricity.

Worked Examples: Balancing Equations and Predicting Reactions

• Law of Conservation of Mass: Atoms must be equal on both sides.
• Balancing Step: Use coefficients, never change the small subscripts.
• Displacement Rule: More reactive metals displace less reactive ones.
• Predicting: Check the Reactivity Series before assuming a reaction.
• No Reaction: Occurs when a less reactive metal meets a more reactive salt.
