Magnetic Flux: Concept, Mathematical Definition, and Vector Nature

• Magnetic Flux (Phi) measures the total magnetic field lines passing through an area.
• Mathematical Formula: Phi = B · A = B A cos(theta).
• The Area Vector (A) is always perpendicular to the surface plane.
• Maximum Flux occurs when B is parallel to the Area Vector (theta = 0).
• Zero Flux occurs when B is perpendicular to the Area Vector (theta = 90 degrees).

Faraday's Laws of Induction: Experimental Evidence and Mathematical Formulation

• Induction requires a change in magnetic flux, not just a magnetic field.
• Faraday's First Law: Changing flux induces an electromotive force (EMF).
• Faraday's Second Law: Induced EMF is proportional to the rate of flux change.
• Mathematical Formula: EMF = - (delta phi / delta t).
• Flux change can occur via field strength, area, or orientation changes.

Lenz's Law: Conservation of Energy and Determining Induced Current Direction

• Lenz's Law determines the direction of induced current.
• Induced current opposes the change in magnetic flux.
• Increasing flux leads to an opposing magnetic field.
• Decreasing flux leads to a reinforcing magnetic field.
• Lenz's Law is a direct consequence of Conservation of Energy.

Motional Electromotive Force (EMF): Derivation and Lorentz Force Connection

• Motional EMF occurs when a conductor moves through a uniform magnetic field.
• Moving charges in a magnetic field experience the Lorentz Force: F = q(v x B).
• Charge separation creates an internal electric field that opposes the magnetic force.
• Derivation: Induced EMF (epsilon) = B * L * v.
• EMF is proportional to magnetic field strength, conductor length, and velocity.

Eddy Currents: Formation, Applications, and Minimization Techniques

• Eddy currents are circulating loops of current induced in solid conductors.
• They cause Joule heating and electromagnetic braking due to Lenz's Law.
• Applications: Induction furnaces, metal detectors, and magnetic braking.
• Disadvantage: Unwanted energy loss and heating in transformers.
• Minimization: Use of laminated cores and high-resistivity materials.

Inductance: Self-Inductance Concept, Coefficient (L), and Derivations

• Self-Inductance: The property to oppose changes in current flow.
• Mathematical Relation: Magnetic Flux (Phi) = L times Current (I).
• SI Unit of Inductance: The Henry (H).
• Derivation for Solenoid: L equals mu naught times N squared times A divided by length.
• Effect of Core Material: Inductance increases with higher permeability.

Mutual Inductance: Concept, Coefficient (M), and Coupling Coefficient

• Mutual Induction: EMF induced in one coil due to changing current in another.
• Coefficient of Mutual Inductance (M): Phi Two equals M times I One.
• SI Unit of M: The Henry (H).
• Coupling Coefficient (k): Ratio of M to the square root of (L1 times L2).
• Range of k: 0 <= k <= 1 (where k=1 is perfect coupling).

Energy Stored in an Inductor: Derivation and Magnetic Field Energy

• Inductors store energy in the form of a magnetic field.
• Work done to increase current is converted into magnetic energy.
• Derivation: Energy U equals one-half L times I squared.
• Energy density is B squared divided by two times mu-naught.
• Stored energy is proportional to the square of the current.

Electromagnetic Induction in AC Generators: Working Principle and Mathematical Analysis

• AC Generator: Converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
• Key Components: Permanent magnet, rotating armature, slip rings, and brushes.
• Working Principle: Changing magnetic flux induces a sinusoidal EMF.
• Mathematical Derivation: E equals N B A omega sine of omega t plus phi.
• Peak EMF: E-max equals N B A omega; depends on N, B, A, and rotation speed.
