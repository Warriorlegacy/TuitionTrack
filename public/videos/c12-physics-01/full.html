Introduction: The Mystery of Charge, Static Electricity, and Real-World Applications

• Electric charge is an intrinsic property of matter, similar to mass.
• Static electricity arises from the imbalance of electrons between surfaces.
• Protons carry positive charge; electrons carry negative charge.
• Real-world uses: Photocopying, air purification, and biological signaling.
• This chapter explores how charges create electric fields and exert forces.

Electric Charge: Properties, Quantization, Conservation, and Conductors vs Insulators

• Like charges repel; unlike charges attract
• Additivity: Total charge is the algebraic sum of individual charges
• Conservation: Charge cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system
• Quantization: Q = n e, where n is an integer and e = 1.6 x 10^-19 C
• Conductors have free electrons; Insulators have tightly bound electrons

Coulomb's Law: Vector Form, Superposition Principle, and Mathematical Derivations

• Coulomb's Law in vector form: F = (k * q1 * q2 / r^3) * r-vector
• Vector notation accounts for both magnitude and direction of force
• Superposition Principle: Net force is the vector sum of individual forces
• Forces are calculated independently as if other charges were absent
• Use component method (sine/cosine) for adding non-parallel force vectors

Electric Field Intensity: Concept, Field Lines, and Field due to a Point Charge

• Electric Field (E) is force per unit positive test charge: E = F/q
• Electric Field is a vector quantity; direction follows a positive test charge
• Field due to point charge Q: E = (1 / 4πε₀) * (Q / r²)
• Field lines start at positive charges and end at negative charges
• Field lines never intersect; density indicates field strength

Electric Dipole: Dipole Moment, Axial and Equatorial Field Derivations

• Electric Dipole: Two equal and opposite charges separated by distance 2a
• Dipole Moment (p) = q times 2a; Direction: Negative to Positive charge
• Axial Field: E = 2kp / r^3 (Direction is along the dipole axis)
• Equatorial Field: E = -kp / r^3 (Direction is opposite to dipole moment)
• Both fields follow the inverse cube law (1/r^3) for short dipoles

Torque on a Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field and Dipole in Non-Uniform Fields

• Net force on a dipole in a uniform field is zero.
• Torque magnitude: Tau equals p times E times sine theta.
• Vector form of torque: Tau equals p cross E.
• Maximum torque occurs when dipole is perpendicular to the field.
• Non-uniform fields exert both net force and torque on a dipole.

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Electric Flux: Concept, Area Vector, and Gauss's Law Statement and Proof

• Area Vector: Magnitude is area, direction is normal to the surface
• Electric Flux (Phi): The dot product of Electric Field (E) and Area Vector (A)
• Phi = E A cos(theta): Flux is maximum when field is perpendicular to surface
• Gauss's Law: Total flux through a closed surface equals Q divided by epsilon naught
• Gauss's Law Proof: Integration of E dot dA over a sphere yields q/epsilon naught

Application of Gauss's Law I: Field due to an Infinitely Long Straight Wire

• Linear charge density lambda = charge per unit length
• Gaussian surface: Coaxial cylinder of radius R and length L
• Flux through end caps is zero because E is parallel to the surface
• Total flux through curved surface is E times 2 pi R L
• Final Formula: E = lambda / (2 * pi * epsilon naught * R)

Application of Gauss's Law II: Field due to Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet

• Surface charge density sigma equals total charge divided by area
• Electric field lines are perpendicular to the infinite plane sheet
• Gaussian surface: A cylinder piercing through the charged sheet
• Flux through the curved surface of the cylinder is zero
• Electric field E equals sigma divided by two epsilon naught

Part 10 of 12 · ~4 min

Application of Gauss's Law III: Field due to Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell

• Gaussian surface: Concentric sphere of radius r
• Outside (r > R): E = Q / (4 * pi * epsilon naught * r^2)
• Outside behavior: Identical to a point charge at the center
• Inside (r < R): Enclosed charge is zero, so E = 0
• Field discontinuity occurs at the surface r = R

Worked Examples: Solving Complex Numerical Problems and Avoiding Common Calculation Pitfalls

• Always treat Electric Field as a vector, not a scalar.
• Convert all units to SI: Coulombs, Meters, and Newtons per Coulomb.
• Direction matters: Negative charges attract field lines, positive charges repel.
• Use component resolution (sin and cos) for multiple charge systems.
• Avoid the 'Approximation Trap' by checking if Gauss's Law assumptions apply.
