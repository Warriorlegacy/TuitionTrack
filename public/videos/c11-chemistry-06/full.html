Introduction: The Concept of Dynamic Equilibrium and Real-World Significance

• Chemical reactions can be unidirectional or reversible
• Static Equilibrium: No movement or change occurs
• Dynamic Equilibrium: Forward and backward rates are equal
• At equilibrium, concentrations of reactants and products remain constant
• Equilibrium is a fundamental concept in biology and industrial chemistry

Reversible Reactions and the Law of Mass Action: Derivations and Expressions

• Irreversible reactions proceed in one direction only.
• Reversible reactions allow reactants to reform from products.
• Law of Mass Action: Rate is proportional to reactant concentrations.
• Derivation: K-c equals the ratio of forward to backward rate constants.
• The value of K-c depends on temperature and reaction stoichiometry.

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

The Equilibrium Constant (Kc and Kp): Mathematical Relationships and Calculations

• Kc: Ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations
• Kp: Ratio of product partial pressures to reactant partial pressures
• Relationship: Kp = Kc times R raised to the power of delta n
• delta n = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants)
• Note: Pure solids and liquids are excluded from equilibrium expressions

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

Le Chatelier's Principle: Qualitative Effects of Concentration, Pressure, and Volume

• Le Chatelier's Principle: System shifts to counteract applied stress.
• Concentration: Adding reactants shifts equilibrium toward products.
• Pressure: Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward fewer gas moles.
• Volume: Increasing volume shifts equilibrium toward more gas moles.
• Note: Pressure changes only affect reactions with different gas moles.

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

Temperature Dependence of Equilibrium: The Van't Hoff Equation and Thermal Effects

• Temperature is the only factor that changes the value of the equilibrium constant K.
• Exothermic reactions: Increasing temperature decreases the equilibrium constant K.
• Endothermic reactions: Increasing temperature increases the equilibrium constant K.
• Van't Hoff Equation: ln(K2/K1) = (-delta H / R) * (1/T2 - 1/T1).
• A plot of ln(K) versus 1/T yields a slope of -delta H / R.

Ionic Equilibrium I: Electrolytes, Degree of Dissociation, and Ostwald's Dilution Law

• Electrolytes: Substances that dissociate into ions and conduct electricity
• Strong Electrolytes: Nearly 100 percent dissociation (e.g., NaCl)
• Weak Electrolytes: Partial dissociation (e.g., CH3COOH)
• Degree of Dissociation (alpha): Fraction of solute that breaks into ions
• Ostwald's Dilution Law: alpha is inversely proportional to the square root of concentration

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Ionic Equilibrium II: Acids and Bases (Arrhenius, Brønsted-Lowry, and Lewis Theories)

• Arrhenius: Acids produce H+ ions; Bases produce OH- ions in water.
• Brønsted-Lowry: Acids are proton donors; Bases are proton acceptors.
• Conjugate Pairs: Species differing only by the presence of one proton.
• Lewis Theory: Acids are electron-pair acceptors; Bases are electron-pair donors.
• Evolution: Lewis theory is the most comprehensive of the three models.

Part 8 of 12 · ~5 min

Ionic Equilibrium III: pH Scale, Ionization Constants (Ka/Kb), and Water Autoprotolysis

• Autoprotolysis: 2 H2O ⇌ H+ + OH-
• Ionic Product of Water: Kw = [H+][OH-] = 10^-14 at 25°C
• pH Scale: pH = -log[H+] and pOH = -log[OH-]
• Relationship: pH + pOH = 14
• Dissociation Constants: Ka for acids and Kb for bases

Ionic Equilibrium IV: Salt Hydrolysis and the Common Ion Effect

• Common Ion Effect: Suppresses ionization of weak electrolytes
• Strong Acid + Strong Base salts result in neutral pH (pH = 7)
• Strong Acid + Weak Base salts produce acidic solutions
• Weak Acid + Strong Base salts produce basic solutions
• Weak Acid + Weak Base salts depend on Ka and Kb ratios

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Ionic Equilibrium V: Solubility Product (Ksp) and Precipitation Criteria

• K-sub-sp is the equilibrium constant for sparingly soluble salts.
• Stoichiometric coefficients become exponents in the K-sub-sp expression.
• Solubility (s) is the molar amount dissolved; K-sub-sp is the constant.
• Ionic Product (Q-sub-sp) determines the current state of the solution.
• Precipitation occurs only when Q-sub-sp is greater than K-sub-sp.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Complexities in Ionic Equilibrium: Buffer Solutions and Indicators

• Buffer Solutions: Resist pH changes upon addition of small amounts of acid or base.
• Acidic Buffer: Weak acid + its salt with a strong base (e.g., CH3COOH + CH3COONa).
• Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation: pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]).
• Indicators: Weak organic acids/bases that change color based on H-ion concentration.
• End Point: The pH at which the indicator undergoes a visible color change.

Exam Masterclass: Problem-Solving Strategies, Common Pitfalls, and Full Chapter Recap

• Identify equilibrium type: Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous
• Always write balanced equations and omit solids/pure liquids
• Distinguish between Reaction Quotient (Q) and Equilibrium Constant (K)
• Apply Le Chatelier's Principle by analyzing system response
• Use Ka/Kb for weak electrolytes; avoid strong acid formulas
