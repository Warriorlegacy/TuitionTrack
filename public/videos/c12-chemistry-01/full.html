Introduction: Why Solutions Matter, Types of Solutions, and Concentration Terms

• Solution: A homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent
• Types: Gaseous, Liquid, and Solid solutions
• Mass Percentage: (Mass of solute / Total mass of solution) x 100
• Mole Fraction: Ratio of moles of one component to total moles
• Molarity (M): Moles of solute per liter of solution (Temperature dependent)

Solubility of Solids in Liquids: Factors Affecting Solubility, Henry's Law, and its Application

• Solubility: Maximum solute amount in a solvent at specific T and P
• Like Dissolves Like: Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents
• Temperature Effect: Solid solubility usually increases with temperature
• Henry's Law: P = K subscript H times C (Solubility proportional to partial pressure)
• Applications: Carbonated drinks, Scuba diving (the bends), and High-altitude anoxia

Solubility of Gases in Liquids: Factors, Henry's Law Derivation, and Real-world Problems

• Gas solubility decreases as temperature increases.
• Henry's Law: Solubility is proportional to partial pressure.
• Formula: C = K subscript H times P.
• High K subscript H values indicate lower gas solubility.
• Applications: Aquatic life, carbonated drinks, and scuba diving.

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

Vapour Pressure of Liquid Solutions: Raoult's Law for Volatile Solutes and Ideal vs Non-Ideal Solutions

• Raoult's Law: Partial pressure is proportional to mole fraction
• Total Pressure = P_A(x_A) + P_B(x_B)
• Ideal Solutions: Obey Raoult's Law, Delta H = 0, Delta V = 0
• Positive Deviation: Weaker A-B interactions, higher vapour pressure
• Negative Deviation: Stronger A-B interactions, lower vapour pressure

A 3D split-screen animation showing molecules in an ideal mixture (uniform movement) versus a positive deviation mixture (molecules flying apart rapidly due to weak attraction).

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

Non-Ideal Solutions: Deviations from Raoult's Law and Azeotropes

• Ideal solutions follow Raoult's Law: A+B interactions equal A-A and B-B
• Positive Deviation: Higher vapour pressure, Delta H is positive, Delta V is positive
• Negative Deviation: Lower vapour pressure, Delta H is negative, Delta V is negative
• Azeotropes: Constant boiling mixtures that cannot be separated by distillation
• Minimum Boiling Azeotropes result from large positive deviations

A split-screen 3D animation showing molecules being pushed apart in positive deviation and pulled closer together in negative deviation, transitioning to a distillation flask showing azeotropic boiling.

Colligative Properties I: Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure and Molecular Mass Determinant

• Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles.
• Non-volatile solutes decrease the vapour pressure of a solvent.
• Relative lowering of vapour pressure is the ratio: (P-zero minus P) / P-zero.
• Relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the mole fraction of the solute.
• This property allows for the determination of an unknown solute's molar mass.

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Colligative Properties II: Elevation of Boiling Point (Ebullioscopic Constant and Derivation)

• Boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure
• Elevation in boiling point: Delta T subscript b = T solution - T pure solvent
• Ebullioscopic Constant (K subscript b) is solvent-specific
• Formula: Delta T subscript b equals K subscript b multiplied by molality (m)
• Molar mass calculation: M = (mass of solute * K subscript b) / (mass of solvent * Delta T subscript b)

Part 8 of 12 · ~5 min

Colligative Properties III: Depression of Freezing Point (Cryoscopic Constant and Applications)

• Freezing occurs when vapor pressure of liquid equals vapor pressure of solid
• Adding non-volatile solute lowers the freezing point of the solvent
• Formula: Delta T f = K f multiplied by molality (m)
• K f is the Cryoscopic Constant (molal depression constant)
• Applications: De-icing roads with salt and using antifreeze in radiators

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

Colligative Properties IV: Osmotic Pressure, Isotonic Solutions, and Reverse Osmosis

• Osmosis: Solvent flow through a semi-permeable membrane.
• Osmotic Pressure (Pi) = Molarity (M) x R x Temperature (T).
• Isotonic solutions have equal osmotic pressures.
• Hypertonic solutions cause cell shrinkage; Hypotonic cause swelling.
• Reverse Osmosis (RO) uses external pressure to purify water.

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Abnormal Molar Masses: Van't Hoff Factor (i), Dissociation, and Association Phenomena

• Abnormal molar mass occurs due to dissociation or association of solute particles.
• Dissociation increases particle count, leading to i > 1 and lower observed molar mass.
• Association decreases particle count, leading to i < 1 and higher observed molar mass.
• Van't Hoff Factor (i) = (Actual number of particles) / (Theoretical number of particles).
• i = 1 for non-electrolytes; i > 1 for dissociation; i < 1 for association.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Worked Examples: Step-by-Step Problem Solving for All Concentration and Colligative Terms

• Step 1: Convert mass to moles and volume to liters for Molarity.
• Henry's Law: Use P = K_H * x to find solubility or mole fraction.
• Raoult's Law: Total Pressure = P_A*x_A + P_B*x_B for volatile solutes.
• Colligative Properties: Always use molality or mole fraction.
• Van't Hoff Factor: Multiply by 'i' for electrolytes (dissociation/association).
