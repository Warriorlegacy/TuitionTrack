The Coming of European Traders: Mercantilism, Competition, and Trade Rivalries

• Mercantilism: Accumulating wealth through gold, silver, and high-profit trade.
• High-demand Indian goods: Cotton, silk, pepper, cloves, and cardamom.
• Key European Rivals: Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French companies.
• Impact of Competition: Rising prices of goods and increased trade rivalry.
• Shift in Strategy: From simple trading to building forts and naval warfare.

East India Company Begins Trade in Bengal: From Factories to Fortification

• The term 'factory' referred to fortified warehouses for storing trade goods.
• The Company sought tax exemptions to maximize profits from silk and cotton.
• Nawabs of Bengal insisted on tax payments to protect state revenue.
• Fortification of settlements was seen as a challenge to local sovereignty.
• Conflict arose between Company commercial interests and Nawab's political authority.

The Battle of Plassey: Causes, Key Figures, and the Turning Point

• Tensions rose as the Company refused to pay taxes and fortified settlements.
• Sirajuddaulah captured Fort William in response to Company defiance.
• Robert Clive led the Company forces to reclaim control in Bengal.
• The betrayal by Mir Jafar was the decisive factor in the battle.
• Plassey transformed the Company from traders into political kingmakers.

The Battle of Buxar and the Grant of Diwani Rights

• Mir Qasim's resistance against Company trade misuse
• The 1764 Battle of Buxar: Company vs. Triple Alliance
• Defeat of the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II
• Grant of Diwani Rights: Control over Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha
• The shift from importing gold to using Indian revenue

Part 6 of 12 · ~4 min

Expansion of Company Rule: Subsidiary Alliance and its Implications

• Subsidiary Alliance: A tool for political expansion without constant war
• Rulers had to disband their own armies and pay for British troops
• Failure to pay resulted in the loss of Indian territories to the Company
• British Residents were stationed in courts to influence internal affairs
• Implication: Indian rulers lost sovereignty and became dependent puppets

• Mysore's economic threat: Control over spices and sandalwood trade
• Strategic alliance: The Mysore-French connection alarmed the British
• The Tiger of Mysore: Tipu Sultan's modernization and resistance
• The Fourth Anglo-Mysore War: The Fall of Seringapatam in 1799
• Consequences: Direct British control and the Subsidiary Alliance

Part 8 of 12 · ~3 min

War with the Marathas and the Doctrine of Lapse

• Maratha power was a confederacy led by the Peshwa
• Three Anglo-Maratha wars led to the crushing of Maratha power
• Lord Dalhousie introduced the Doctrine of Lapse
• States without natural male heirs were annexed by the Company
• Satara, Sambalpur, Nagpur, and Jhansi were annexed via this policy

A 3D map of India where Maratha territories shrink through three war phases, followed by a red overlay spreading across Satara and Jhansi as the Doctrine of Lapse is explained.

Part 9 of 12 · ~3 min

Setting up a New Administration: Courts, Collector, and Judicial Systems

• Administrative units were divided into three Presidencies: Bengal, Madras, and Bombay
• Each Presidency was headed by a Governor to manage regional authority
• Two types of courts were established: Faujdari Adalat (Criminal) and Diwani Adalat (Civil)
• The Collector became the key official responsible for revenue and law and order
• The new judicial system often prioritized Company interests over local customs

The Company Army: Recruitment, Training, and Technological Shifts

• Shift from cavalry (horsemen) to infantry (foot soldiers)
• Recruitment of peasants known as 'Sepoys' or 'Sipahis'
• Use of modern firearms: Muskets and Matchlocks
• Emphasis on professional training and disciplined formations
• Transition from traditional warfare to organized military machines
