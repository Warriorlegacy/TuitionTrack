Introduction: Real-world Applications, Importance, and Classification of Alcohols & Phenol

• Alcohols contain a hydroxyl group (O-H) attached to a saturated carbon.
• Phenols feature a hydroxyl group attached directly to an aromatic ring.
• Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary alcohols are classified by carbon attachment.
• Monohydric, Dihydric, and Trihydric alcohols are classified by O-H count.
• Real-world uses: Ethanol in sanitizers, Phenols in antiseptics and resins.

Nomenclature and Structure: IUPAC Naming, Isomerism, and C-O-H Bond Geometry

• IUPAC: Longest chain must include the carbon attached to the -OH group
• Naming Ethers: Smaller group is 'alkoxy', larger is the parent alkane
• Isomerism: Alcohols and Ethers are functional group isomers
• Metamerism: Different alkyl group distributions around the oxygen in ethers
• Geometry: Oxygen is sp3 hybridized with a bent shape due to two lone pairs

Methods of Preparation I: From Alkenes (Acid-catalyzed Hydration & Hydroboration-Oxidation)

• Acid-catalyzed Hydration: Follows Markovnikov's Rule
• Hydration Mechanism: Formation of carbocation intermediates
• Risk of Rearrangement: Carbocations can undergo shifts
• Hydroboration-Oxidation: Yields Anti-Markovnikov alcohols
• Regioselectivity: Choosing the right method for the target alcohol

Part 4 of 12 · ~4 min

Methods of Preparation II: From Carbonyl Compounds (Grignard Reagent & Reduction)

• Grignard Reagent (R-Mg-X) acts as a strong nucleophile.
• Formaldehyde + Grignard yields Primary Alcohols.
• Other Aldehydes + Grignard yield Secondary Alcohols.
• Ketones + Grignard yield Tertiary Alcohols.
• LiAlH4 is a strong reducer; NaBH4 is a selective reducer.

Part 5 of 12 · ~4 min

Physical Properties: Hydrogen Bonding, Boiling Points, and Solubility Trends

• O-H bond polarity leads to strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
• Alcohols have higher boiling points than alkanes of similar molar mass.
• Boiling point increases with alkyl chain length due to Van der Waals forces.
• Small alcohols are miscible in water due to hydrogen bonding with H2O.
• Solubility decreases as the hydrophobic alkyl chain length increases.

Part 6 of 12 · ~4 min

Chemical Properties of Alcohols I: Reaction with Metals, HX, and Dehydration Mechanisms

• Reaction with metals produces metal alkoxides and hydrogen gas.
• Reaction with HX follows SN1 or SN2 pathways based on alcohol class.
• Primary alcohols react via SN2; tertiary alcohols react via SN1.
• Dehydration of alcohols requires an acid catalyst and heat.
• Saytzeff's Rule: More substituted alkenes are the major products.

Part 7 of 12 · ~3 min

Chemical Properties of Alcohols II: Oxidation (Stepwise) and Esterification

• Primary alcohols oxidize to aldehydes, then to carboxylic acids.
• Secondary alcohols oxidize to form ketones.
• Tertiary alcohols are resistant to oxidation under normal conditions.
• PCC is a mild reagent used to stop oxidation at the aldehyde stage.
• Esterification: Alcohol + Carboxylic Acid -> Ester + Water (Acid Catalyst).

Part 8 of 12 · ~4 min

Phenols: Preparation Methods (from Cumene, Benzene Sulfonic Acid, and Diazonium Salts)

• Cumene oxidation: Produces Phenol and Acetone as a valuable byproduct
• Benzene Sulfonic Acid: Requires alkali fusion with molten NaOH
• Diazonium Salts: Prepared from aniline via diazotization at 0-5 degrees Celsius
• Hydrolysis of Diazonium Salts: Warming with water yields phenol and nitrogen gas
• Industrial vs Lab scale: Cumene for mass production, Diazonium for precision

A split-screen 3D animation showing: 1. Cumene transforming into Phenol and Acetone, 2. Benzene Sulfonic Acid reacting with molten NaOH, 3. A Diazonium salt molecule releasing N2 gas to become Phenol.

Part 9 of 12 · ~3 min

Acidity of Phenols: Electronic Effects, Comparison with Alcohols, and Substituent Effects

• Phenols are more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilization
• Phenoxide ion delocalizes negative charge into the benzene ring
• Alkoxide ions lack resonance, making them less stable than phenoxide ions
• Electron-Withdrawing Groups (EWG) increase acidity (e.g., Nitro group)
• Electron-Donating Groups (EDG) decrease acidity (e.g., Methyl group)

A 3D molecular model showing the movement of electron density from the oxygen atom into the benzene ring via resonance arrows.

Part 10 of 12 · ~3 min

Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution: Nitration, Bromination, and Kolbe/Reimer-Tiemann Reaction

• Phenol is highly reactive towards Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution
• Nitration: Dilute nitric acid gives nitrophenols; Conc. acid gives Picric Acid
• Bromination: Bromine water yields 2,4,6-tribromophenol precipitate
• Kolbe-Schmidt Reaction: Phenol plus Carbon Dioxide yields Salicylic Acid
• Reimer-Tiemann Reaction: Phenol plus Chloroform yields Salicylaldehyde

Part 11 of 12 · ~3 min

Ethers: Preparation (Williamson Synthesis) and Physical Properties

• Williamson Synthesis: Nucleophilic substitution (S-N-2) using sodium alkoxide and alkyl halide
• Crucial Rule: Use primary alkyl halides to prevent elimination and alkene formation
• Boiling Points: Lower than alcohols due to lack of intermolecular hydrogen bonding
• Dipole-Dipole Forces: Ethers have higher boiling points than alkanes due to polar C-O bonds
• Solubility: Small ethers are water-soluble by accepting hydrogen bonds from water
