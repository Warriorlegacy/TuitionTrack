Introduction: Real-world applications and the concept of Inequality vs Equality

• Equality represents a perfect balance: x equals a specific value.
• Inequality represents a range: one value is greater or less than another.
• Symbols: < (less than), > (greater than), <= (less than or equal to), >= (greater than or equal to).
• Real-world use: Budgets, speed limits, and temperature ranges.
• Goal: Finding a set of possible solutions rather than a single number.

Part 2 of 12 · ~5 min

Foundations: Algebraic rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing inequalities

• Addition and subtraction do not change the inequality sign.
• Multiplying/dividing by a positive number keeps the sign the same.
• Multiplying/dividing by a negative number REVERSES the inequality sign.
• Rule: If a < b and c is negative, then ac > bc.
• Always check the sign of the coefficient before dividing.

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

The Golden Rule: Handling negative coefficients and reversing inequality signs

• Adding or subtracting values does not change the inequality sign.
• Multiplying or dividing by a positive number keeps the sign the same.
• CRITICAL: Multiplying/dividing by a negative number reverses the sign.
• Example: If -2x > 8, then x < -4 after dividing by -2.
• Always check your final solution on a number line to verify direction.

Linear Inequalities in One Variable: Solving and representing on a Number Line

• Isolate the variable using standard algebraic operations
• Remember to flip the inequality sign when dividing by a negative
• Use a solid circle for inclusive inequalities (<= or >=)
• Use an open circle for strict inequalities (< or >)
• Shade the number line to represent the infinite solution set

Graphical Representation: Solving inequalities in two variables on a Cartesian plane

• Use dashed lines for < or > and solid lines for <= or >=
• The line divides the Cartesian plane into two half-planes
• Use the Test Point Method (usually origin 0,0) to check regions

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Shading Regions: Testing points and determining the feasible half-plane

• A linear inequality divides the Cartesian plane into two half-planes.
• The Test Point Method identifies which half-plane contains the solutions.
• The origin (0,0) is the simplest test point if it is not on the line.
• If the test point satisfies the inequality, shade its side.
• If the test point fails, shade the opposite side of the boundary line.

Systems of Linear Inequalities: Finding the common solution region (Intersection)

• A system consists of two or more linear inequalities.
• The solution is the intersection of all individual shaded regions.
• A point must satisfy every inequality in the system to be a solution.
• Solid lines for inclusive boundaries (<= or >=), dashed for strict (< or >).
• The overlapping area is often referred to as the Feasible Region.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Common Pitfalls: Identifying sign errors, boundary mistakes, and interval notation errors

• Sign Error: Flip the sign ONLY when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.
• Addition/Subtraction: Never flip the sign when adding or subtracting negatives.
• Boundary Mistake: Use dashed lines for strict inequalities (<, >) and solid for (<=, >=).
• Interval Notation: Use parentheses ( ) for excluded endpoints and brackets [ ] for included.
• Infinity Rule: Always use a parenthesis ( ) when writing infinity or negative infinity.
