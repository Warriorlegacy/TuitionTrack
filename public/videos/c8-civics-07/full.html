Introduction: Why Public Facilities Matter and the Concept of Common Goods

• Public Facilities: Essential services required for a dignified life.
• The Concept of Common Goods: Resources shared by the entire community.
• Non-excludable and Non-rivalrous: Key characteristics of shared resources.
• Social Equality: Ensuring access to facilities regardless of income.
• Government Responsibility: The duty to provide and manage public services.

The Role of the Government: Why Private Companies Cannot Provide All Facilities

• Private companies operate primarily on the 'Profit Motive'.
• Profit-seeking can lead to 'Exclusion' of low-income or remote groups.
• The Government ensures 'Universal Access' as a matter of social justice.
• Large-scale infrastructure often forms 'Natural Monopolies'.
• Public facilities are often funded by taxes to ensure affordability.

Water: A Fundamental Right, Access Issues, and the Cost of Provision

• Water is recognized as a fundamental human right for survival and dignity.
• Physical access gaps: Many households lack direct pipeline connections.
• Water quality issues: Contamination leads to serious water-borne diseases.
• Economic access: Balancing the cost of infrastructure with affordability.
• Cross-subsidization: Using higher commercial rates to subsidize poor households.

Sanitation and Health: Public Hygiene, Waste Management, and Social Impact

• Sanitation is a critical pillar of public health and disease prevention.
• Public hygiene involves maintaining clean streets, drains, and public spaces.
• Effective waste management follows the principle of Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle.
• Poor sanitation disproportionately affects marginalized communities.
• Sanitation access is a matter of social dignity and gender equality.

• The Electricity Grid: Generation, Transmission, and Distribution.

Healthcare and Education: Public vs. Private Provision and the Right to Life

• Public Provision: Government-run, tax-funded, focused on social welfare.
• Private Provision: Individually owned, profit-driven, often high-cost.
• The Divide: High costs in private sectors can exclude the poor.
• Article 21: The Right to Life includes the right to health and education.
• Government Obligation: Ensuring dignity and access for all citizens.

The Economics of Public Facilities: Pricing, Subsidies, and Affordability

• Cost of Production: Capital costs and operational expenses of services.
• The Dilemma: Balancing cost recovery with social affordability.
• Subsidies: Using tax revenue to lower prices for essential services.
• Cross-Subsidization: Charging higher rates to industries to support households.
• Equity in Pricing: Ensuring fair access through progressive pricing models.

Common Mistakes: Misconceptions about Government Roles and Resource Allocation

• Misconception: Public facilities are 'free' (Reality: They are tax-funded).
• Misconception: Government only handles large-scale infrastructure.
• Misconception: Spending on public facilities is a waste of resources.
• Misconception: Private efficiency always replaces the need for government.
• Misconception: Resource allocation means equal distribution to everyone.
