Introduction: Real-world Applications and Why Metals and Non-metals Matter

• Matter is composed of elements classified as Metals or Non-metals
• Metals: Essential for construction, electronics, and infrastructure
• Non-metals: Vital for life processes, breathing, and nutrition
• Classification is based on unique physical and chemical properties
• Understanding these elements is key to modern engineering and biology

Physical Properties: Detailed Analysis of Metals and Non-metals with Activities

• Metals are typically lustrous, while non-metals are generally dull (Exception: Iodine)
• Malleability: Metals can be beaten into sheets; Non-metals are brittle
• Ductility: Metals can be drawn into wires; Non-metals cannot
• Conductivity: Metals conduct heat and electricity well (Exception: Graphite)
• State: Most metals are solid (Exception: Mercury); Non-metals vary

Chemical Properties I: Reaction of Metals with Air and Water

• Metals react with oxygen to form basic metal oxides.
• Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame to form magnesium oxide.
• Highly reactive metals like Sodium are stored under kerosene.
• Reactivity with water: Cold water vs. Hot water vs. Steam.
• Hydrogen gas is released during the reaction of metals with water.

Chemical Properties II: Reaction of Metals with Acids and Salt Solutions

• Metals + Dilute Acid -> Metal Salt + Hydrogen Gas
• The 'Pop' Test confirms the presence of Hydrogen gas
• Nitric acid often produces water instead of Hydrogen gas
• Displacement: More reactive metal replaces less reactive metal
• Example: Iron displaces Copper from Copper Sulphate solution

The Reactivity Series: Understanding Metal Potency and Displacement Reactions

• Reactivity Series: A hierarchy of metals based on their chemical potency.
• High Reactivity: Potassium and Sodium (found only in compounds).
• Low Reactivity: Copper, Silver, and Gold (found in free state).
• Displacement Reaction: More reactive metal replaces a less reactive metal.
• Example: Zinc + Copper Sulfate yields Zinc Sulfate + Copper.

Ionic Compounds: Formation, Electron Dot Structures, and Properties

• Ionic compounds form via electron transfer from metals to non-metals.
• Electrostatic force of attraction holds cations and anions together.
• Electron Dot Structures represent valence electrons visually.
• Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points due to strong bonds.
• Electrical conductivity occurs only in molten or aqueous states.

Metallurgy I: Occurrence of Metals and Concentration of Ores

• Minerals: Naturally occurring substances in the Earth's crust.
• Ores: Minerals from which metals can be extracted profitably.
• Gangue: The unwanted impurities like sand and clay in an ore.
• Gravity Separation: Uses density differences to remove impurities.
• Froth Floatation: Uses pine oil to separate sulphide ores.

Part 8 of 12 · ~4 min

Metallurgy II: Extraction of Metals (Low, Medium, and High Reactivity)

• Low reactivity metals: Extracted by heating or roasting sulphide ores.
• Medium reactivity metals: Converted to oxides via roasting or calcination.
• Reduction of medium reactivity oxides: Uses Carbon or Coke as a reducing agent.
• High reactivity metals: Cannot be reduced by carbon; require electrolysis.
• Electrolytic reduction: Metal ions move to the cathode to gain electrons.

A 3D split-screen showing a furnace for roasting, a chemical reduction setup, and an electrolytic cell with glowing ions moving to electrodes.

Part 9 of 12 · ~4 min

Refining of Metals: Electrolytic Refining and Purification Processes

• Refining is necessary to remove impurities from extracted metals.
• Electrolytic Refining: Uses an electrolyte and two electrodes.
• Anode: Thick block of impure metal; Cathode: Thin strip of pure metal.
• Oxidation at Anode: Copper loses electrons to become Copper Two Plus ions.
• Reduction at Cathode: Copper ions gain electrons to become pure Copper metal.

A 3D cross-section of an electrolytic cell showing copper ions migrating from a dissolving impure anode to a growing pure cathode, with anode mud accumulating at the bottom.

Part 10 of 12 · ~4 min

Corrosion: Mechanisms, Prevention, and Alloys

• Corrosion: Gradual destruction of metals by air, moisture, and chemicals.
• Rusting of Iron: Formation of hydrated iron oxide (iron oxide of two times water).
• Prevention: Painting, oiling, and galvanization (coating with zinc).
• Alloys: Homogeneous mixtures of metals or metals and non-metals.
• Examples: Bronze (Copper + Tin), Brass (Copper + Zinc), Stainless Steel (Iron + Cr + Ni).
