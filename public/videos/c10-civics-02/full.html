Defining Federalism: Key Features and the Dual Polity System

• Two or more tiers of government (Union and State)
• Specific jurisdictions defined by the Constitution
• Fundamental provisions require mutual consent to change
• Courts act as an umpire to resolve disputes
• Dual Polity: Citizens follow both Central and State laws

Unitary vs. Federal Systems: Comparative Analysis and Distinctions

• Unitary System: Central government holds supreme and absolute power.
• Federal System: Power is constitutionally divided between Center and States.
• Sub-units in Unitary systems are subordinate to the central authority.
• Federalism requires a written constitution to protect state autonomy.
• Unitary focuses on uniformity; Federalism focuses on regional diversity.

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

Coming Together vs. Holding Together Federations: Detailed Comparison

• Coming Together: Independent states join to form a larger unit.
• Coming Together Examples: USA, Switzerland, and Australia.
• Holding Together: A large country divides power to maintain unity.
• Holding Together Examples: India, Spain, and Belgium.
• Key Difference: Equal power in Coming Together vs. Unequal power in Holding Together.

What Makes India a Federal Country? The Constitutional Provisions

• India is described as a 'Union of States', not a federation by agreement.
• Three-tier system: Union, State, and Local governments.
• Union List: National importance (Defense, Banking, Currency).
• State List: Local importance (Police, Agriculture, Trade).
• Concurrent List: Common interest (Education, Marriage); Union law prevails in conflict.

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

The Three Lists: Union, State, and Concurrent Lists Explained

• Union List: National importance, only Central Government makes laws
• State List: Regional importance, only State Governments make laws
• Concurrent List: Common interest, both Centre and State can make laws
• Conflict Rule: Union law prevails over State law in the Concurrent List
• Examples: Defense (Union), Police (State), Education (Concurrent)

Residuary Powers and Special Provisions for Certain States

• Residuary Powers: Subjects not in Union, State, or Concurrent lists.
• Parliament holds exclusive power over Residuary subjects (e.g., IT, AI).
• Special Provisions: Articles 371 to 371-J protect regional identities.
• Protects customary laws and land rights in North-Eastern states.
• Balances national unity with regional cultural diversity.

How Federalism is Practiced: Linguistic States and Language Policy

• Linguistic states were created to ensure administrative ease and unity.
• Reorganizing states by language strengthened Indian democracy.
• India has no single 'National Language'; Hindi is an 'Official Language'.
• The Eighth Schedule recognizes 22 Scheduled Languages.
• Flexible language policy prevents conflict and protects linguistic diversity.

Centre-State Relations: Evolution from One-Party Dominance to Coalition Era

• One-party dominance: Congress ruled both Centre and most States for decades.
• Impact of dominance: Reduced state autonomy and increased central influence.
• Rise of regional parties: Shifted the political landscape in the 1980s and 90s.
• Coalition Era: National parties needed regional allies to form governments.
• Strengthened Federalism: Increased negotiation and respect for state powers.

Decentralization in India: The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments

• Decentralization: Transferring power from Central/State to Local governments.
• 1992 Amendments: 73rd (Rural) and 74th (Urban) Constitutional changes.
• Mandatory regular elections and reservation for SC, ST, and women.
• Panchayati Raj: Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, and Zila Parishad.
• Urban Bodies: Municipalities for towns and Municipal Corporations for cities.
