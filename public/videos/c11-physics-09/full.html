Introduction: Why Fluids Matter, Pressure, and Pascal's Law

• Fluids include both liquids and gases because they flow under shear stress.
• Pressure is defined as Force divided by Area (P = F/A).
• The SI unit of pressure is the Pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m squared.
• Pascal's Law: Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally.
• Hydraulic systems use Pascal's Law to multiply force using different areas.

Fluid Statics: Variation of Pressure with Depth and Atmospheric Pressure

• Pressure in a static fluid increases linearly with depth
• Formula: P equals P-zero plus density times g times h
• Pressure depends on density and depth, not container shape
• Atmospheric pressure decreases as altitude increases
• Gauge pressure is the difference between absolute and atmospheric pressure

Part 3 of 12 · ~4 min

Pascal's Law Applications: Hydraulic Machines and Transmission of Pressure

• Pascal's Law: Pressure change is transmitted undiminished in enclosed fluids.
• Hydraulic Press Formula: F-two equals F-one times the ratio of A-two to A-one.
• Force Multiplication: Large area pistons produce much larger output forces.
• Conservation of Energy: Small distance moved by large piston vs large distance by small.
• Real-world uses: Hydraulic brakes, car lifts, and heavy construction machinery.

Surface Tension: Molecular Theory, Surface Energy, and Surface Tension Formula

• Cohesive forces: Inward pull on surface molecules
• Surface Tension: Force per unit length (T = F/L)
• Surface Energy: Work done to increase surface area
• Formula: Surface Tension = Surface Energy / Area
• SI Units: N/m or J/m^2; Temperature decreases tension

Capillarity: Capillary Rise, Surface Tension in Tubes, and Surface Tension Forces

• Capillary action: liquid movement in narrow tubes due to surface tension
• Adhesion vs Cohesion: determines if a liquid wets a surface or not
• Contact angle (theta): determines capillary rise or depression
• Jurin's Law: h equals two T cosine theta divided by rho g r
• Height of rise is inversely proportional to the tube radius

Fluid Dynamics: Streamline vs Turbulent Flow and Equation of Continuity

• Streamline Flow: Particles follow smooth, predictable paths.
• Turbulent Flow: Irregular, chaotic motion with eddies and swirls.
• Conservation of Mass: Mass entering a system equals mass leaving it.
• Equation of Continuity: A-sub-one times v-sub-one equals A-sub-two times v-sub-two.
• Inverse Relationship: Smaller area leads to higher fluid velocity.

Bernoulli's Principle: Derivation, Energy Conservation, and Applications

• Bernoulli's Principle is an application of the Law of Conservation of Energy.
• Ideal fluids must be non-viscous, incompressible, and in streamline flow.
• Total energy per unit volume = Pressure + Kinetic Energy + Potential Energy.
• An increase in fluid velocity results in a simultaneous decrease in pressure.
• The principle holds true along a single streamline in a flowing fluid.

Part 8 of 12 · ~4 min

Real-world Applications of Bernoulli's Principle: Venturi Meter and Torricelli's Law

• Venturi Meter: Uses pressure difference in a constriction to measure flow rate
• Bernoulli's link: Higher velocity in narrow sections leads to lower pressure
• Torricelli's Law: Calculates the velocity of fluid exiting an orifice
• Formula for efflux: Velocity equals the square root of two g h
• Torricelli's result: Efflux velocity equals free-fall velocity from height h

Viscosity: Newton's Law of Viscosity, Coefficient of Viscosity, and Velocity Gradient

• Viscosity: Internal friction resisting the relative motion of fluid layers.
• Velocity Gradient: The rate of change of velocity with respect to distance (dv/dy).
• Newton's Law of Viscosity: F is proportional to the velocity gradient.
• Coefficient of Viscosity (eta): The proportionality constant of viscous force.
• SI Unit: Pascal-second (Pa-s); CGS Unit: Poise (1 Pa-s = 10 Poise).

Stokes' Law and Terminal Velocity: Derivations and Drag Forces

• Stokes' Law: F = 6 * pi * eta * r * v
• Three forces acting on a falling sphere: Weight, Buoyancy, and Viscous Drag
• Terminal Velocity occurs when Net Force equals zero
• Derivation: Weight = Buoyancy + Viscous Drag
• Terminal Velocity formula: v-t = (2/9) * [r^2 * g * (rho_s - rho_f)] / (eta * rho_f)

Part 11 of 12 · ~4 min

Reynolds Number and Flow Regimes: Predicting Fluid Behavior

• Laminar flow: Smooth, predictable, and parallel layers.
• Turbulent flow: Chaotic, irregular, and characterized by eddies.
• Reynolds Number (Re) = (density * velocity * diameter) / viscosity.
• Low Re (< 2000): Viscous forces dominate, resulting in laminar flow.
• High Re (> 4000): Inertial forces dominate, resulting in turbulent flow.
