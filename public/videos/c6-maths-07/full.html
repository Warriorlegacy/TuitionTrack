Introduction: Why Fractions Matter in Real Life & The Concept of Parts of a Whole

• Fractions represent parts of a whole object or a collection.
• Crucial Rule: The parts must be of equal size to be considered fractions.
• Real-life examples: Sharing food, telling time, and measuring ingredients.
• A fraction describes how many parts we have out of the total parts.
• Fractions allow us to be precise when dealing with quantities less than one.

Understanding Numerators and Denominators: Visualizing Fractions with Diagrams

• Numerator: The number of parts we are considering
• Denominator: The total number of equal parts in a whole
• Denominator starts with 'D' for 'Division'
• All parts in a fraction diagram must be equal in size
• Fraction = Numerator / Denominator

Representing Fractions on a Number Line: Step-by-Step Construction

• Draw a straight horizontal line with marks for 0, 1, 2, etc.
• Identify the denominator to determine the number of equal divisions.
• Divide the space between whole numbers into equal segments.
• Use the numerator to count the number of segments from zero.
• Mark the final position with a dot and label the fraction.

Types of Fractions I: Proper, Improper, and Mixed Fractions Explained

• Proper Fraction: Numerator < Denominator (Value < 1)
• Improper Fraction: Numerator >= Denominator (Value >= 1)
• Mixed Fraction: A combination of a Whole Number and a Proper Fraction
• Example: 3/4 is Proper; 7/4 is Improper; 1 and 3/4 is Mixed
• Improper and Mixed fractions represent the same quantities

Part 5 of 12 · ~5 min

Converting Between Improper Fractions and Mixed Fractions: Methods and Examples

• Improper to Mixed: Divide numerator by denominator
• The quotient is the whole number, the remainder is the new numerator
• Mixed to Improper: Multiply whole number by denominator
• Add the numerator to that product to get the new numerator
• Always keep the denominator the same in both conversions

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Types of Fractions II: Equivalent Fractions and Finding Missing Values

• Equivalent fractions represent the same value or part of a whole.
• To find equivalent fractions, multiply or divide numerator and denominator by the same number.
• Example: 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8.
• To find missing values, identify the multiplier or divisor used in the known parts.
• Rule: Whatever you do to the top, you must do to the bottom!

Simplifying Fractions: Reducing to the Lowest Terms using HCF

• Simplifying means reducing a fraction to its lowest terms.
• Lowest terms: Numerator and denominator have no common factors except 1.
• Step 1: Find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of the numerator and denominator.
• Step 2: Divide both the numerator and denominator by the HCF.
• Example: 15/25 simplifies to 3/5 using HCF of 5.

Comparing and Ordering Fractions: Like Denominators vs. Unlike Denominators

• Like Denominators: Compare only the numerators.
• Unlike Denominators: Find a common denominator first.
• Use the Least Common Multiple (L.C.M.) for efficiency.
• Convert fractions to equivalent forms before comparing.
• Larger numerator with same denominator means a larger fraction.

Part 9 of 12 · ~5 min

Fractional Parts of a Collection: Applying Fractions to Sets of Objects

• The 'whole' refers to the total number of objects in a set.
• Denominator = Total number of objects in the collection.
• Numerator = Number of objects in the specific group being counted.
• The sum of all fractional parts must equal one whole.
• Always simplify the fraction to its lowest terms for the final answer.

Part 10 of 12 · ~5 min

Addition and Subtraction of Like Fractions: Concepts and Worked Examples

• Like Fractions have the same denominator.
• Addition: Add numerators, keep the denominator the same.
• Subtraction: Subtract numerators, keep the denominator the same.
• Never add or subtract the denominators.
• Always simplify your final answer to the lowest terms.

A 3D chocolate bar splitting into equal segments that move together to form a larger group during addition and separate during subtraction.

Part 11 of 12 · ~5 min

Addition and Subtraction of Unlike Fractions: Using LCM Method

• Unlike fractions have different denominators and cannot be added directly.
• Step 1: Find the Least Common Multiple (L.C.M.) of the denominators.
• Step 2: Convert fractions into equivalent fractions using the L.C.M.
• Step 3: Add or subtract the numerators while keeping the denominator the same.
• Always simplify your final answer to the lowest terms if possible.
