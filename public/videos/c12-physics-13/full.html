Introduction: The Microscopic World, Scale of Nuclei, and Why Nuclear Physics Matters

• The atom is mostly empty space; the nucleus is a tiny, dense core.
• Atomic scale: Angstroms (10^-10 m) vs. Nuclear scale: Femtometers (10^-15 m).
• 1 Fermi is equal to 10 to the power of negative fifteen meters.
• Nuclear energy is derived from the strong force holding nucleons together.
• Applications: Energy production, medical imaging, and cosmic evolution.

Composition of Nucleus: Atomic Number, Mass Number, Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

• Nucleons: Protons (positive charge) and Neutrons (neutral charge)
• Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus
• Mass Number (A): Total number of protons and neutrons (A = Z + N)
• Isotopes: Same Z (protons), different A (mass)
• Isobars: Same A (mass), different Z (protons)

Part 3 of 12 · ~5 min

Nuclear Size, Radius, and Density: Derivations and Experimental Evidence

• Nuclear radius follows the empirical law: R = R0 * A^(1/3)
• R0 (nuclear radius parameter) is approx 1.2 femtometers
• Radius increases with the cube root of the mass number A
• Nuclear density is independent of the mass number A
• Nuclear density is constant at approx 2.3 x 10^17 kg/m^3

Part 4 of 12 · ~5 min

Mass Defect and Nuclear Binding Energy: The Concept of Mass-Energy Equivalence

• Mass Defect (delta m) = (Sum of nucleon masses) - (Actual nuclear mass)
• Mass-Energy Equivalence: E = m-c-squared
• Nuclear Binding Energy = delta m times c-squared
• Binding Energy per Nucleon = Total Binding Energy / Mass Number (A)
• Higher Binding Energy per Nucleon implies greater nuclear stability

Binding Energy per Nucleon Curve: Stability, Fission, and Fusion Trends

• Binding Energy per Nucleon = Total Binding Energy / Mass Number (A)
• The curve peaks at Iron-56, representing maximum nuclear stability
• Light nuclei (low A) undergo Fusion to increase binding energy
• Heavy nuclei (high A) undergo Fission to increase binding energy
• Nuclear stability is determined by the energy released during transitions

Part 6 of 12 · ~5 min

Nuclear Forces: Characteristics, Properties, and Short-Range Nature

• Extremely strong: Overcomes electrostatic proton repulsion
• Short-range: Effective only up to approximately 2 femtometers
• Charge-independent: Same strength for p-p, n-n, and p-n pairs
• Repulsive at very short distances: Prevents nuclear collapse
• Spin and non-central: Depends on nucleon orientation and spin

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

Radioactivity Part I: Alpha, Beta, and Gamma Decay Mechanisms and Laws

• Alpha Decay: Emission of a Helium nucleus (2 protons, 2 neutrons)
• Beta-minus Decay: Neutron converts to proton; emits electron and antineutrino
• Beta-plus Decay: Proton converts to neutron; emits positron and neutrino
• Gamma Decay: Emission of high-energy photons from excited nuclei
• Radioactivity is a spontaneous, random, and statistical process

Part 8 of 12 · ~5 min

Radioactivity Part II: Radioactive Decay Law, Half-Life, and Mean Life Derivations

• Decay rate is proportional to the number of nuclei: dN/dt = -lambda * N
• Radioactive Decay Law: N = N0 * e^(-lambda * t)
• Half-Life formula: T half = 0.693 / lambda
• Mean Life formula: tau = 1 / lambda
• Relationship: Half-life is approximately 1.44 times the mean life

Radioactive Decay Applications: Carbon Dating, Medical Isotopes, and Activity Calculations

• Carbon-14 dating uses the decay of organic matter to determine age.
• Radiocarbon half-life is approximately 5,730 years.
• Medical tracers like Technetium-99 help in non-invasive organ imaging.
• Radioisotopes like Cobalt-60 are used in cancer radiotherapy.
• Activity A equals lambda times N; measured in Becquerels (Bq).

Worked Examples: Solving Complex Decay, Binding Energy, and Mass Defect Problems

• Mass Defect: Delta M = (Sum of nucleon masses) - (Actual nuclear mass)
• Binding Energy: E = Delta M multiplied by 93.15 MeV
• Binding Energy per Nucleon: Total Binding Energy divided by Mass Number A
• Decay Law: Activity N equals N naught times two to the power of minus t/T half

Common Pitfalls: Misconceptions in Decay Laws, Units, and Mathematical Errors

• Distinguish between Activity (A), Decay Constant (lambda), and Half-life (T 1/2)
• Use Exponential Decay Law: N = N zero times e to the power of negative lambda t
• Convert Mass Defect from atomic mass units (u) to MeV using 931.5 MeV/u
• Stability is determined by Binding Energy per Nucleon, not total Binding Energy
• Precision matters: Avoid premature rounding and verify Isotope/Isobar/Isotone definitions
