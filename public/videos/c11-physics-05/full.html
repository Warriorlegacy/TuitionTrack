Introduction: The Concept of Work, Scalar Product, and Dot Product Derivations

• Work requires both Force and Displacement.
• Work is zero if displacement is zero or perpendicular to force.
• Scalar Product (Dot Product): A dot B = A * B * cos(theta).
• Work formula: W = F dot d = F * d * cos(theta).
• Positive work (0°), Zero work (90°), Negative work (180°).

Work Done by a Constant Force vs Variable Force: Integration and Area under F-x Graphs

• Constant Force: Work equals Force multiplied by Displacement
• Variable Force: Force changes magnitude or direction during motion
• Calculus Approach: Work is the integral of Force with respect to x
• Graphical Approach: Work equals the area under the F-x curve
• Sign Convention: Positive area for force in direction; negative for opposing

Kinetic Energy, Work-Energy Theorem, and Derivations for Constant/Variable Forces

• Kinetic Energy (K) = 1/2 m v squared
• Work-Energy Theorem: W_net = Delta K
• Derivation for Constant Force: W = 1/2 m v^2 - 1/2 m u^2
• Variable Force: Integral of F(x) dx = Delta K
• Work-Energy Theorem applies to both constant and variable forces

Potential Energy: Gravitational Potential Energy and Spring Potential Energy Derivations

• Potential energy is defined only for conservative forces.
• Gravitational Potential Energy: U = mgh (relative to a reference point).
• Hooke's Law: Restoring force F = -kx.
• Spring Potential Energy derivation: Integral of kx dx = 1/2 kx^2.
• Elastic potential energy is always positive due to the x-squared term.

Conservative vs Non-Conservative Forces: Path Independence and Field Properties

• Conservative Force: Work done is independent of the path taken.
• Closed Loop Property: Work done by conservative forces in a cycle is zero.
• Non-Conservative Force: Work done depends on the length and shape of the path.
• Dissipative Nature: Non-conservative forces like friction convert energy to heat.
• Potential Energy: Only exists for conservative forces via a potential field.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy: Vertical Motion and Pendulum Systems

• Mechanical Energy E = Kinetic Energy K + Potential Energy U
• In a conservative field, the total mechanical energy remains constant
• Vertical Motion: K decreases as U increases during ascent
• Simple Pendulum: Energy oscillates between K and U
• Height of pendulum bob h = L(1 - cos theta)

Part 7 of 12 · ~5 min

The Concept of Power: Instantaneous vs Average Power and Efficiency Calculations

• Power is the rate of doing work: P = Work / Time
• SI Unit of Power: Watt (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s
• Instantaneous Power: P = dW/dt or P = F dot v
• Efficiency (η) = (Useful Output / Total Input) x 100%
• Real-world machines always have efficiency less than 100%

Collisions in One Dimension: Elastic vs Inelastic Collisions and Coefficient of Restitution

• Momentum is conserved in all one-dimensional collisions.
• Elastic Collisions: Both Momentum and Kinetic Energy are conserved.
• Inelastic Collisions: Momentum is conserved, but Kinetic Energy is lost.
• Perfectly Inelastic Collisions: Objects stick together after impact.
• Coefficient of Restitution (e) = Relative velocity of separation / Relative velocity of approach.

Collisions in Two Dimensions: Vector Analysis and Scattering Problems

• Momentum conservation applies to x and y axes independently
• Vector components: m-one v-one cos(theta) and m-one v-one sin(theta)
• Elastic collisions require conservation of kinetic energy
• Scattering angle depends on the impact parameter
• Impact parameter is the perpendicular distance of approach

• Use Free-Body Diagrams to identify all force components before calculating work.

Common Pitfalls: Sign Conventions, Directional Errors, and Conceptual Misunderstandings

• Work sign depends on the angle: positive if theta < 90, negative if theta > 90
• Potential energy is relative; always define a zero-reference level first
• Net Work equals change in Kinetic Energy, not just work from one force
• In 2D collisions, resolve momentum into independent x and y components
• Zero work does not mean zero force; check if force is perpendicular to motion
